Turn 2 · emblems, not glyphs

Two emblems built on the lore.

The idea underneath the name: you cannot know how far away something is until you have moved. Astronomers get a star's distance by looking at it twice, six months apart, from opposite ends of Earth's orbit. Depth is what patience and displacement buy you. That is why these issues are structural rather than reactive — and it is a far better thing to put in a mark than a pair of squares.

Each emblem below is a composed group where every element carries something, and each comes with its reduction ladder — seal, device, glyph — because the dense version is for covers and the glyph is for a browser tab.

2a The Six-Month Baseline an observatory seal

Everything in the seal is load-bearing. The dotted ellipse is one orbit; the two ink squares on it are the same observer in January and in July. The two sightlines cross at the accent object, carry on, and land on the fixed field as two different positions — the ring in accent is where the object appears in winter, the ink ring where it appears in summer, and the dimension between them is the whole argument.

The six squares on the fixed field are the six desks, standing in for the distant stars that never appear to move. The publication's promise is that ring-to-ring gap: the same object, twice, and the depth that only shows up because you waited.

Ring legend: PARALLAX LENS · THE SAME OBJECT, SIX MONTHS APART. It is a stamp for covers, the About page, the colophon, subscriber post — the places a publication signs its name.
