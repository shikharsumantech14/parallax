# The asserted half and the drawn half

**A panel finding of the form "the issue asserts X three times and draws it
zero times, while drawing not-X twice" is two findings, and only one of them is
a prose job.** Say which one you closed.

**Why:** the reader's complaint ("a careful reader can finish believing the
opposite of the premise") is a *contradiction* on the page, and a contradiction
is wording. The underlying defect is that the argument has no mark pointing the
asserted way, and a stylist may not add a graphic or a figure. Reporting
"closed" for both invites a second panel read that finds the same gap.

**How to apply.** Three moves close the contradiction, all inside fields the
stylist owns:

1. **Scope the drawn half instead of denying it.** Replace an absolute
   ("Nobody raised a price") with an acknowledgement plus its scope ("One price
   rose, at the top of the market. Where the price per piece held still, the
   bill rose, because …"). The reader stops on an absolute they have seen
   contradicted; they do not stop on a scoped one. Denying causation
   ("that rise is not what emptied the budget") is the tempting version and it
   is usually an unsourced causal claim — scope the *place*, not the *cause*.
2. **Promote the "nothing changed" data point into the evidence.** Most issues
   of this shape carry one figure where a cost moved while a price did not.
   Re-label it so it reads as that ("Cost rise with the price per piece
   unchanged", not "Cost rise at an unchanged rate card"). It is the only mark
   on the page that can carry the asserted half, and tile labels are usually
   writable when chart labels are not.
3. **Give the assertion a concrete object once**, in the gloss where it first
   appears ("one chat reply costs less than it used to"), rather than repeating
   the abstraction. Concrete is not evidence, and the summary should not claim
   it is.

**What stays open, and say so:** the issue still has no mark pointing the
asserted way. Name the unblock the storyboard already priced (usually a
declined kind in §8d plus a research pass) so the operator sees a costed door
rather than a gap.

## The vocabulary chain is what makes the scoping land

The scoped sentence only works if it reuses the exact words the issue taught.
When the gloss section defines a term ("a token is a piece of a word", "the
per-token price is what one of those pieces costs"), the repair sentence and
the tile label must both say **price per piece** — not a third synonym. A third
name for a glossed thing ("rate card") is what the panel reads as trade
language; killing it from the tile removes the need to gloss it in the closer
as well, so one edit pays twice.

## "Say in a sentence what the two bars already show" — and the ratio trap

The other common panel ask is the inverse: a comparison the reader must do by
eye ("Indonesia's own fossil emissions are smaller than India's, yet the fire
alone hits 38%") exists only as two bar widths. Writing it out is squarely a
prose job.

**The trap is that the dossier usually supplies a ratio word for it, and the
ratio word usually disagrees with the drawn values.** On the Indonesia issue
the dossier's prose said Indonesia's fossil emissions are "a fifth" of India's
while the values the chart draws (221 and 870) are a quarter. Both are sourced;
picking either publishes a number the page contradicts, and recomputing is
research, not styling.

**Say the comparison without the ratio** — "India's band is the widest, and
Indonesia's own is a fraction of it". The reader gets the relation the panel
asked for, the two widths still supply the size, and nothing on the page
disagrees with anything else. Flag the dossier/drawn mismatch for the verifier
rather than resolving it.
