# The two nearest noun phrases bind, and on some subjects that is not survivable

**In any data card whose subject is a harm done TO the people the issue is
about, name the victim as a grammatical object in the sentence and give the
penalty its unit. Spend headroom here before anywhere else.**

**Why:** a statutory penalty compresses into a noun phrase very naturally
("abduction with mutilation or castration to force a transgender identity
brings ten years to life"), and the compression is what breaks it. At scrolling
speed the reader binds the two nearest noun phrases: *a transgender identity*
and *ten years to life*. A reader on the reader panel made exactly that bind,
corrected herself on a second pass from the card title, and the panel would not
ship the line. "Ten years to life" also never says **of what** — the most
literal reader asked it outright. On a subject where the misreading is "these
people get ten years", the misreading may not be available at all.

**How to apply:**
- Put the victim into the sentence as an object, twice if the grammar allows:
  *"to force a transgender identity on the person abducted"*. A victim who is
  only implied by the verb is not named.
- Give the number its unit in the same clause: *ten years **in prison**, up to
  life*. Every figure stays character for character.
- Split into two sentences at the penalty. The offence is one sentence, the
  punishment is the next. It costs about 13 words and it removes the bind
  entirely, because the penalty now has its own subject.
- Do NOT reach for "is now an offence" / "this protects X" to fix it. Both are
  claims the dossier may not carry (the act was usually an offence already; a
  penal clause "protecting" someone is a characterisation). Faithful grammar
  beats an added verb.
- This overruns the storyboard's per-card word cap by roughly half. Say both
  numbers in the summary; the cap is the budget, not the ceiling, and the
  launching agent will have priced it.
