# Cutting the redundancy makes the Hindi word load-bearing

**If a reader or a panel says a Hindi word "reads as placed rather than spoken"
because the English beside it already says the same thing, tightening the
sentence is the wrong fix.** The redundancy *is* the compliance mechanism.

**Why:** the skip test (contract §2, test 1) passes only because deleting the
Hindi phrase leaves a complete English sentence. On the asteroid closer,
*"It is still up there, in the same aasmaan, on the same path it has always
flown"* passes because "up there" carries the location. Remove "up there" to
kill the repetition and *aasmaan* becomes the only sky-word in the sentence,
which is rule 1 (Hindi is never load-bearing) broken. There is no third
phrasing: a noun standing in for an English noun is either duplicated or
load-bearing.

**How to apply:** when handed the choice "keep it and cut the redundancy, or cut
it", say out loud that the first option does not exist, and then choose between
keeping it as authored and cutting it. On a word the operator has already ruled
in (a storyboard §8c ruling), keep it as authored and hand the operator the
zero-Hindi alternate the storyboard usually carries beside it. `aasmaan` in the
2024 YR4 closer was kept on exactly this reasoning, 2026-09-15.

**The corollary for the space desk:** the lexicon licenses *aasmaan* and little
else there, so a space issue's single Hindi word is nearly always in the closer,
nearly always beside "up there" or "out there", and nearly always the only one
a panel will interrogate. Expect to defend it rather than to place more.
