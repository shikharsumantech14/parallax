## C-01 · Half of India is short of rain in the decade it got wetter

- **status:** open
- **why now:** IMD began withdrawing the southwest monsoon from West Rajasthan
around 19 September 2026, closing a season it had already downgraded on
29 May to 90% of the long period average with a 60% chance of being outright
deficient. By 27 July the all-India figure was 16% short and about 53% of
districts were deficient or large-deficient. The season's final number lands
on 30 September.
- **angle:** The all-India monsoon number is one figure standing in for a
country, and it is hiding the actual event. CEEW's tehsil-level work finds
55% of India's tehsils got *more* monsoon rain over 2012–2022, a quarter of
them more than 30% extra, while around 40% of districts now swap between
drought years and flood years. So 2026's "deficit" is not the monsoon
weakening. It is the monsoon being re-sorted underneath a national average
that cannot show it. Then the second turn: the "normal" the deficit is
measured against is itself a number IMD rewrites. The rainfall LPA is now
87 cm on a 1971–2020 base, and IMD has moved its climate baselines four
times (1961–1990, then 1971–2000 in 2016, 1981–2010 in 2019, 1991–2020 in
  1. — a revision Down To Earth calculates understated 2024's warming by
  about 0.54°C. A shortfall is a subtraction, and India keeps changing the
  number it subtracts from.
- **suggested kinds (drawn graphics first):**
  - `attrition-waffle` [i] — **never published.** 100 squares, one per 100
  Indian districts, grouped by IMD's own rainfall categories (large
  deficient / deficient / normal / excess / large excess) for the 2026
  season. Data: district rainfall category counts. Source: **IMD Rainfall
  Statistics** (`mausam.imd.gov.in/responsive/rainfall_statistics.php`),
  with the 53%-deficient figure cross-checked against Down To Earth. The
  kind demands counts summing to exactly 100 plus the real `trueN` (~730
  districts) — the honesty chip is the point here.
  - `region-map` — **never published.** India's 36 meteorological

  Data: subdivision-wise seasonal departure table. Source: **IMD Rainfall
  Information** (`rainfallinformation_swd.php`). ⚠ The component is a
  Natural Earth 50m world SVG keyed on zone `id`; whether Indian

## C-02 · The Arctic now sets its records in March, not September

- **status:** open
- **why now:** The annual Arctic sea-ice minimum falls in mid-September, and
NSIDC's 3 September 2026 analysis says a new record low is highly unlikely
this year — August extent ranked around twelfth lowest. Six months earlier,
on 15 March 2026, the Arctic hit its winter maximum at 14.29 million km², a
statistical tie with 2025's 14.31 million for the lowest maximum in the
48-year satellite record. Two record-low winters in a row, and an
unremarkable summer.
- **angle:** Every year the world waits for the September number, because
September is when the ice is smallest and the picture is most alarming. That
is the wrong month to watch. Carbon Brief, reporting the March figure, notes
the winter maximum has declined relatively steadily for forty years while
the September minimum's record still stands from 2012, because summer extent
is knocked around by one season's weather. The measurement everyone
publicises is the noisy one. The quiet one — how much ice the ocean can
still make in the dark — has been failing on schedule, and February 2026
Arctic ice volume was the second lowest on record. The reveal: an
unremarkable minimum is not good news, it is a reading taken with the wrong
instrument.
- **suggested kinds (drawn graphics first):**
  - `climate-spiral` [i] — **never published.** Monthly Arctic sea-ice extent,
  one loop per year, 1979–2026, month scrub. Data: `{months:[{year, month, value}]}` in million km². Source: **NSIDC Sea Ice Index** monthly extent
  (G02135, `nsidc.org/data/seaice_index`). The spiral is the whole argument
  in one figure: the March arc collapsing inward faster than the September
  arc.
  - `region-map` — **never published.** Per-sea ice-extent anomaly against the
  1981–2010 mean for the marginal seas that drove the 2026 maximum (Barents,
  Kara, Bering, Okhotsk, Greenland Sea). Data: regional monthly extent
  anomalies. Source: **NSIDC Sea Ice Index** regional series; cross-check
  **NOAA Arctic Report Card** sea-ice chapter.
  - `margin-bullets` [i] — **never published.** Four to eight Arctic
  measurements, each on its own scale against its own reference: March
  maximum against the 1981–2010 mean, September minimum against the same,
  February volume against its record, multi-year ice fraction, mean
  thickness. Mixed units are exactly this kind's case, and the build fails
  on a missing unit or range so every row must be captured whole.
  - `climate-strip` — the annual March maximum, one stripe per year,
  1979–2026, annotated at 2025 and 2026. Same NSIDC source.
  - Support, not counted as drawn: `number-sense` (1.36 million km² of missing
  winter ice, converted to something Indian — roughly two-fifths of India's
  land area; the arithmetic must be stated and checked, not asserted).

## C-03 · Indonesia's fire is burning the soil, not the trees

- **status:** chosen
- **why now:** Carbon Brief reported on 11 September 2026 that Indonesian
fires had already released 76 MtC by 7 September and are, in Guido van der
Werf's words, "more or less on track" to match 2015 — the year 2.6 million
hectares burned and 333 MtC went up. Mongabay counts nearly 286,000 hectares
burned across Sumatra and Borneo between January and 24 August, haze
crossing into Malaysia, 50,891 acute respiratory infection cases logged by
Indonesia's health ministry in July and August, and 72 people charged.
- **angle:** A forest fire burns what is standing. A peat fire burns what the
forest spent thousands of years putting underground. Nisa Novita of YKAN
puts the mechanism plainly: drained peat "can smolder underground, so the
soil itself becomes readily available fuel" — which is why Indonesia's fire
emissions are an order of magnitude out of line with its burned area, and
why the fire is not out when the flames are. The structural turn is that
this is a plumbing decision, not a weather event: the carbon only burns
because the water table was lowered by drainage canals dug for plantations,
and El Niño, as Novita says, is "a major amplifier," not a cause. Indonesia
built a peatland restoration agency after 2015 and dissolved it in 2025.
- **suggested kinds (drawn graphics first):**
  - `core-sample` — **never published.** A peat column by depth: the metres of
  accumulated carbon under one hectare, where the drained water table now
  sits, and how far down the smouldering front reaches. Data:
  `{layers:[{depth, label, value}], unit}` — peat depth class and carbon
  density per layer. Source: **Global Forest Watch** peat-depth and
  soil-carbon layers (`globalforestwatch.org`), with the mechanism anchored
  in **IPCC SRCCL** (`ipcc.ch/srccl/`). ⚠ This is the candidate's riskiest
  graphic; if a real sourced core profile cannot be had, `elevation-profile`
  (also never published) on the same vertical structure is the fallback.
  - `region-map` — **never published.** Burned area or fire-alert count per
  Indonesian province for 2026 (Riau, Jambi, South Sumatra, West / Central /
  South Kalimantan, Papua), with the peat provinces called out. Data:
  per-province burned hectares and alert counts. Source: **Global Forest
  Watch** fire alerts, cross-checked against Mongabay's FireWatch figures.
  - `carbon-loop` — **never published.** The peat stock-and-flow, conservation
  checked: the carbon held in Indonesian peatland, the annual flux out
  through fire, the flux out through drained-peat oxidation with no fire at
  all, and what restoration returns. Data: reservoir and flux values in GtC
  and MtC/yr. Source: **IPCC SRCCL** for the land-carbon accounting,
  **Global Carbon Project** (`globalcarbonbudget.org`) for the land-use
  flux term.
  - `benchmark-chart` — Indonesian fire carbon emissions by year, highlighting
  2026 against 1997 (800+ MtC), 2015 (333 MtC) and the 2000–2026 average
  (~85 MtC). Data: the annual GFED series. Source: reported by **Carbon
  Brief** from the Global Fire Emissions Database; the researcher should
  reach the underlying dataset paper through **ESSD**
  (`essd.copernicus.org`) rather than quote the news figure alone.
  - Support, not counted as drawn: `three-steps` (drain → dry → smoulder).
- **est. read time:** 7 min
- **sources:**
  - [https://www.carbonbrief.org/el-nino-indonesia-fire-emissions-in-2026-on-track-to-match-record-for-this-century](https://www.carbonbrief.org/el-nino-indonesia-fire-emissions-in-2026-on-track-to-match-record-for-this-century) — T4 · `mainstream-science` (11 Sep 2026, Cecilia Keating; 76 MtC, the 2015 and 1997 comparisons, all three expert quotes)

wetter). Reason: it is the only candidate that is both timely to the day — the

successful climate treaty ever signed never mentions climate"). Today is
World Ozone Day and the mechanism is excellent Parallax material, but the

the December window rather than hanging an issue on a 39th anniversary.

carbon (strong mechanism and strongly Indian, but the load-bearing study is

better as the driver inside C-01 than as the subject).
