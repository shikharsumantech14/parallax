## The hook

**It stops the thumb for all four.** "You have been told to watch 1.5°C" is a
"you" and a number the reader has met in headlines, and the reversal arrives
in the next seven words. The title carries the finding on its own, so a
reader who reads nothing else still leaves with the argument.

- **Sana:** yes. The number is familiar and the second sentence contradicts
  it. Her one stumble is the third sentence, "Keep cutting and 1.5°C is
  enough" — "enough" has no object, and she has to borrow "survives" from
  the sentence before to complete it. She completed it. It cost a beat.
- **Aarav:** yes. "1.5°C" is the number he has seen without ever being told
  what it is for, and this hook tells him it is the wrong one to watch.
- **Meera:** yes, and the dek closed it: "The world watches the slower one"
  told her the shape of the piece before she committed.
- **Karthik:** yes. No Hindi to skip anywhere in the head or the body.

**What it lacks:** nothing to picture. Every number in the head is a
temperature. The first concrete thing in the issue (savanna, "dry grass and
scattered trees") arrives in section 2's intro, and the first thing the reader
can stand in (Delhi) arrives in section 7. That is a cost of the 79-of-80-word
ceiling, not a defect, and I am not asking for head words.

**The primer's one weak link:** "the study below" points forward to a study
that is never named until section 5's timeline. A reader who wants to know
whose study this is waits five sections.

## Findings on the seven test points

### 1. The belief inversion — **holds, comfortably**

No reader finished thinking "the Amazon is dying because of climate change".
The inversion is taught in four separate places, and three of them are
unskippable:

- the **title** states it outright and is the single most effective element in
  the draft;
- the **hook** reverses 1.5°C inside twenty-one words;
- **section 1's** two panels put the belief and its correction side by side;
- **section 8's** lead names the lever: "A degree of warming takes every
  nation and decades to move. A hectare takes one line in one country's
  budget."

All four readers retold it unprompted. Aarav's version, "kaatna rokna ek
government ka kaam hai, garmi ke liye poori duniya chahiye", is the storyboard's
§1 sentence back in his own words. This is the draft's real achievement and
nothing in the fix list touches it.

### 2. THE INDIAN GROUND — **no reader formed the barred belief. Watch one path.**

I tested the monsoon sentence four times, deliberately looking for the failure.
**Nobody came away believing that Amazon clearing changes Indian rainfall.**

The reason is structural and it is working: the sentence borrows only the
direction of travel, and the very next sentence relocates the mechanism with an
explicit contrast. "But in the Amazon the forest itself keeps that air wet" is
the guard, and "But" is doing the work. The three step cards that follow are
all Amazon, all Brazil, and India never returns.

**One residual path, and it is worth one edit.** The skim reader who takes the
section title plus the first sentence and nothing else reads:

> "Cutting here kills trees over there" + "You already know half of this from
> the monsoon."

That pair sits one inference from the barred claim, and the relocation is in
the sentence after. Sana is the reader who reads exactly that pair. She did not
make the leap, because "half of this" told her something was being withheld and
she read on to find it. But the guard is one sentence downstream of the
temptation, where it could be in the same sentence.

**Direction, not a rewrite:** put the Amazon inside the sentence that borrows
the monsoon, so the skim path cannot end in India. The current draft also
dropped "the first" from the storyboard's "you already know the first half of
this" — "the first half" named *which* half was shared and *which* was coming.
"Half of this" does not.

### 3. The Delhi comparison — **it lands, and it does not read as bolted on**

Aarav, Sana and Karthik all retold section 7 as "nearly four Delhis", which is
the test. It does not read as a bolted-on Indian reference for one specific
reason: the intro **declares the move** ("in an area you already know") instead
of pretending a connection between Brazil and India. An honest unit swap reads
as a favour to the reader. A fake link would have read as pandering.

Two costs:

- **"in an area you already know" is the section's stumble.** It parses first
  as a *place* ("a region you are familiar with"), and for a moment two readers
  expected the issue to name an Indian forest. The card resolves it, but the
  intro is what lands first.
- **One `equals` row is thin.** The whole Indian ground of the issue rests on a
  reader's feel for Delhi's area. A reader who has never thought about Delhi in
  km² has no second door. The storyboard banked ~101 words of headroom and
  named a second `equals` row as the one thing worth buying (§8g). This is
  where to spend it.

### 4. The honesty devices — **one reads as careful, one reads as confusion**

These split cleanly, and the split is instructive.

**The hero's low-end bars read as careful.** The `plain` line says it in the
reader's words and in the right place: "Both bars are drawn at the low end of
their published range." The caption carries both full bands in the order the
bars appear, so the mapping works without the sublabels. No reader was
troubled. This device is a success and should be copied.

**The dial's 17-against-18 reads as confusion**, and it is not the two numbers
that cause it. It is the sentence:

> "The needle is at 17%, the lower of the two figures the reporters give. The
> other is roughly 17–18%."

The reader is promised two figures and given a figure and a range that
*contains* that figure. That is unfollowable as arithmetic. Meera read it
twice and moved on; Sana skipped the panel; Aarav asked what the second number
was. Then the third sentence, "Both are their framing of today, not a figure
quoted from the paper", refers to reporters who have not been introduced and a
paper that is not named until section 5.

The honesty is right and must survive. The sentence is written for a verifier,
not for a reader on a phone. It is the only place in the draft where the issue
sounds like it is talking to itself.

**Note the render order**, because it decides this. `throughput-dial` is not a
VizCard kind, so `core/Section.astro` puts the authored `howToRead` **above**
the graphic and the `plain` line **below** it. The reader therefore meets the
confusing paragraph *before* the dial, and the sentence that would have
explained the dial arrives *after* it.

### 5. `throughput-dial` — **right shape, mute graphic. The lowest-scoring section.**

The zone does convey nearness, but only for a reader who has already read the
caption. Judged as the reader meets it, three things work against it:

- **It draws no needle.** The component renders a fill arc plus a centre
  readout. The authored `howToRead` says "needle" three words in, so the
  reader's first instruction is to find a thing that is not on the screen.
- **The band carries no number.** The arc prints `0`, `100` and `17`. The
  22–28 zone renders as a short grey segment with a legend reading "the
  tipping band". A reader looking only at the picture cannot say where the
  band is. The number that makes the section mean something is in section 2's
  caption, three sections back.
- **The colour reads backwards.** The fill arc is `var(--accent)`, which on
  earth is the living green, so the share of forest *destroyed* is drawn in
  the desk's most cheerful colour while the danger band is soft grey. The
  storyboard predicted this (§8c.4). The `plain` line corrects it, but the
  `plain` line renders below the graphic, so the correction arrives after the
  misread.

Against that, the 270° arc at `max: 100` is mostly empty track, which whispers
"plenty of room left" at exactly the moment the caption says the opposite.
The caption rescues it because it renders at the top of the card. That is the
graphic being carried by its text rather than carrying it.

**Is a gauge the wrong shape?** No. One reading against a named zone is
precisely this beat, and the storyboard's reasoning holds. It is the wrong
gauge only in the sense that its key value is unlabelled. The storyboard's
recorded fallbacks (§8c.4) are not needed. Naming the band on the graphic and
replacing the word "needle" would move this section from 3 to 4 for all four
readers.

### 6. The hero's invisible sublabels — **survivable, but they were hiding a contradiction**

Judged as the reader meets it, section 2 works. It was the second-highest
scoring section. The caption carries both bands, in bar order, so nothing
sourced is lost.

What the missing sublabels *do* cost is the reconciliation of a contradiction
the reader can see:

- section 1 says the 3.7–4 figure applies "with clearing held where it is
  today";
- section 2's bar for the same figure is labelled **"If the cutting stops"**;
- the sublabel that would have reconciled them ("3.7–4°C, with clearing held
  at today's level") **never renders**.

Held-at-today's-level and stopped are different worlds, and the bar label is
the largest text in the row after the value. Karthik caught it on the first
read and it cost him the only re-read he had in that section. The bar labels
are otherwise the clearest binary in the issue and are doing enormous work, so
this is a wording alignment, not a re-kinding.

### 7. The close — **satisfying, and it does not trail off**

Section 8 lands. Its lead is the best sentence in the issue and it renders.
The argument arrives in the right order: the asymmetry, then the proof that one
side of it moved (Brazil, 2025), then the deadline.

Two observations, neither a defect:

- **It ends in someone else's voice.** The final clause is a quoted absolute
  from Nobre, "it is impossible to save the Amazon". That is a hard landing and
  it earns its place, but the issue's own last word is a citation rather than
  the reader's lever.
- **"Think of the two dials on one machine" arrives last.** The reader has been
  using that metaphor since section 2's intro ("the slow dial", "the fast
  one"). Formally introducing it in the final paragraph reads as a late
  explanation of something already understood. It is not confusing, only
  slightly out of order.
- **The clearest single statement of the argument is invisible by default.**
  The `skimCaption`, "Warming takes a century and every nation. Clearing takes
  one budget and one government, and that is the dial the Amazon answers to
  fastest", renders only in Skim mode. That is the correct field for it and I
  am not asking for it to move. Flagging only so nobody assumes the default
  reader saw it.

## What would fix it (direction only, never the sentence)

Ranked by how many readers each one moves. Every fix names what it comes out
of; none touches the head or section 1, which are at the 79-of-80 ceiling.
The storyboard banked ~101 words of headroom (§8g) and the draft used 964 of
1,100, so items 1 through 6 are free.

1. **Section 4, the `howToRead`: the word "needle" names a thing the component
   does not draw.** `ThroughputDial` renders a fill arc and a centre readout,
   no needle. Name what the eye actually lands on. This single word cost all
   four readers a beat and it is the first instruction they are given.

2. **Section 4: the tipping band needs its number where the eye is.** The arc
   prints 0, 100 and 17; the band prints nothing. The `zones[].label` is
   currently "the tipping band" and it renders as a legend chip, so the number
   can ride there, or the caption can name 22 the way section 2's caption
   already does. Today a reader who looks only at the graphic cannot answer
   Q3's second half.

3. **Section 4: "points" is a term of art used twice and never glossed.** It
   appears in the caption ("Four to five points of forest") and again in
   section 5's intro ("So the gap is four to five points"), while the dial
   itself is labelled in `%`. Aarav and Sana both stopped on it. Gloss it once
   where it first appears, or use the unit the graphic uses.

4. **Section 2: the bar label and section 1's condition describe different
   worlds.** "If the cutting stops" against "with clearing held where it is
   today". Pick one condition for the 3.7 figure and say it the same way in
   both places. The sublabel that used to reconcile them does not render, so
   the page currently offers no way to resolve it.

5. **Section 5: the 2018 threshold (20–25%) needs a word that says it was
   superseded.** The reader is carrying 22–28 from two sections earlier and
   meets 20–25 with only the label "The original threshold" to mark the
   difference. Aarav and Sana both read it as a contradiction before they read
   it as history. One connective in the note settles it.

6. **Section 5: 2.78 million hectares has no comparison and no bridge to the
   5,796 km² two sections later.** It is the issue's largest loss figure and it
   looks smaller than its smaller sibling because the units differ. Either give
   it a feel, or say plainly that the two counts measure different things, so a
   reader who notices the mismatch is not left thinking one of them is wrong.
   Pays out of row 5's six notes, which the storyboard names as the first
   source of slack.

7. **Section 6: "Two of those dates moved the right way" points at an
   ambiguous pair.** Only one timeline event carries the callout that says so.
   Name them, or drop the count. Meera scrolled back, which is the one thing
   she does not do twice.

8. **Section 6: "ecological resilience" and "these systems" are the issue's
   only unglossed jargon**, and they sit inside the follow-up to the issue's
   one quote, where the reader is already holding a second scientist and a
   second study. Sana lost the sentence entirely. The quoted span is verbatim
   and fixed, so the gloss has to come from the frame around it.

9. **Section 8: the first body sentence carries two conditions in one clause.**
   "On heat alone the Amazon is tough, which means the danger arrives early
   only when cutting is added." This is the argument's final statement and it
   is the densest sentence in the close. Split it and both halves are plain.

10. **Section 7: "in an area you already know" reads as a place, not a unit.**
    Two readers expected an Indian forest. The move itself is right, and
    declaring it is why Delhi does not read as bolted on. Only the phrasing
    misdirects.

11. **Section 3: move the guard into the sentence that borrows the monsoon.**
    Not a defect today, a margin. Naming the Amazon inside the borrowing
    sentence closes the one skim path (title plus first sentence) that ends
    near the barred claim. The storyboard's "the first half" was more precise
    than the draft's "half of this" and gave up nothing.

12. **Optional, and the best use of the banked headroom: a second `equals` row
    in section 7.** The issue's entire Indian ground is one comparison to one
    city's area. A second door costs about ten words and the storyboard already
    nominated it (§8g).
