## Findings on the nine test points

### 1. Section 4 — **it works, and the two readings read as care**

This is the biggest move in the pass: 3 / 3 / 3 / 3 → 4 / 5 / 4 / 5.

What fixed it is the **order** the three fields now arrive in, not any one of
them alone. The reader meets the card top down:

1. **caption** — "The reporters put today's clearing at 17%, or roughly
   17–18%." The reader gets both readings in one breath, joined by "or
   roughly", before there is anything to reconcile.
2. **howToRead** — points at two things that exist on screen, the filled arc's
   end and the marked band, and names the distance between them as the
   finding. Then the honesty line.
3. **the figure** — 17% in the centre, "of the forest cleared" under it, the
   band labelled with its own numbers.
4. **plain** — "The arc is drawn at the lower of the two published readings."

**Does the 17-against-18 read as care rather than confusion? Yes, and the
reason is diagnosable.** Last time the reader was promised "two figures" and
handed a figure and a range that contained it, in a panel that arrived before
the graphic. That is unfollowable as arithmetic, so it read as the issue
contradicting itself. Now the spread is stated as one fact in the caption
("17%, or roughly 17–18%"), the caveat says where the figure comes from
("the reporters' reading of today, not a figure quoted from the study"), and
the plain line names the editorial choice ("the lower of the two published
readings"). A stated choice reads as care. An unexplained gap reads as
confusion. The difference is entirely that the choice is now named.

Two supporting details that matter:

- **"The reporters" now has an antecedent one line above the caveat** — the
  caption introduces them. Last pass, "their framing" pointed at nobody.
- **"the study"** is carried from the primer ("the study below measures two
  breaking points"), so it is not an orphan either, and §5 names where it
  landed.

Meera's behaviour is the proof: she read the third howToRead sentence, judged
it a caveat, and skipped it **without losing anything**. That is what a
non-load-bearing honesty note is supposed to do.

**One residual, and it is in the head, not the section.** The hook says

### 7. The barred belief — **held, and the skim path is now closed too**

I ran this harder than any other test, deliberately hunting the failure.
**No reader, at any point in the scroll, believed this forest affects Indian
rainfall.**

What changed is the sentence that carries the borrowing. "The Amazon's rain
starts the way the monsoon does, and you already know that first half" puts
**the Amazon inside the borrowing sentence** and restores "that first half",
which names in advance that only part of the comparison transfers. Last pass
the guard ("But in the Amazon the forest itself keeps that air wet", byte
identical and still doing its job) sat one sentence downstream of the
temptation. Now there are two fences and the first one is in the tempting
sentence itself.

The four paths I tested:

- **Full read.** Subject is "the Amazon's rain"; the monsoon is the object of
  a comparison, never a consequence. No causal direction is stated anywhere.
- **Sana's path (title plus first line).** "Cutting here kills trees over
  there" + "The Amazon's rain starts the way the monsoon does, and you
  already know that first half." The title's object is **trees**, not rain,
  and India has no trees anywhere in this issue. The leap now requires
  inventing a claim the words do not contain.
- **Cards only.** Step 2 says "in parts of the basin", step 3 says "hundreds
  of kilometres away". Both locate the mechanism in the Amazon.
- **Aarav's path.** "Monsoon" is the word he owns best and the one he would
  linger on. "You already know that first half" is an explicit fence before
  he can run with it, and "But in the Amazon" is the second.

The bar holds. No change wanted.

### 8. The unit bridge (2.78 million hectares against 5,796 km²) — **it does not cost a reader, and I would leave it**

The two figures never sit in the same eyeful: they are two sections apart, in
different sections' notes, measuring different things. The clause that
matters, "a wider count than clearing", arrives **attached to the number that
needs it**, in the same sentence, which is where a gloss belongs.

Per reader:

- **Sana and Aarav** do not convert units and never tried. Both read 2.78
  million hectares as "a lot, and it counts more than cutting", which is the
  correct reading. Neither noticed a mismatch.
- **Meera** noticed the unit switch and took the clause as permission not to
  compare. No cost.
- **Karthik** is the only one who converted, and what he arrived at is a
  **question he cannot answer** ("is 2024 five times worse than 2025?"),
  not a contradiction he must resolve. The clause tells him the two counts are
  not the same measure, so he parked it and read on. It cost him no part of
  section 5's meaning, which is "2024 was the fire year".

**Recommendation: do not spend words closing it.** Converting would mean
typing a numeral the record does not carry, which the storyboard's number
ledger (§8e) forbids, and the semantic warning is already in the right place.
The explanation carries the reader past. One reader notices; none is misled.

### 9. Section 1's `actually.text` — **it no longer costs Aarav the re-read, and the available fix is word-negative**

"On heat alone, with clearing held where it is today, the forest holds to this
much warming."

The sentence is unchanged, but **what the reader meets before it** is not.
The caption renders at the top of the card and states the condition once and
plainly: "With clearing held at today's level, the forest holds to 3.7–4°C."
The value "3.7–4 °C" sits above the sentence, so "this much warming" resolves
by pointing rather than by inference. By the time Aarav reaches the panel
text he already holds the fact, so the sentence is a restatement of something
he has, not a gate to something he needs.

What it still costs him is **a beat, not a re-read**: "On heat alone" and
"with clearing held where it is today" are two names for the same condition,
and stacking them makes him check whether a third condition is coming. He
retold the section correctly either way, so it scores 4, and it does not
block PASS.

**On the word-neutrality constraint:** the fix here is word-**negative**, not
word-neutral. The two conditions are one condition said twice, so removing
one gives words back to the 79-of-80 count rather than spending them. If the
operator would rather leave section 1 untouched at this stage, the cost is one
beat for one reader and I would accept it.
