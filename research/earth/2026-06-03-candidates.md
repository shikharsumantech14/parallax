## C-01 · The Line We Will Cross Without Crossing It

- **status:** open
- **why now:** On 28 May 2026 the WMO published its Global Annual to Decadal
  Climate Update 2026–2035 (produced by the UK Met Office). It puts a **91%
  chance that at least one year between 2026 and 2030 temporarily exceeds
  1.5°C** above the 1850–1900 baseline, and an **86% chance** one of those
  years beats 2024 as the warmest on record. Copernicus' April 2026 bulletin
  already had the trailing 12-month mean at ~1.42°C above pre-industrial.
- **angle:** Most readers believe "1.5°C" is a single threshold the planet
  either crosses or doesn't — a wall. The WMO report says the opposite, in
  its own words: the Paris 1.5°C and 2.0°C figures refer to **long-term
  warming "sustained over an extended period, typically assessed over 20
  years."** So the world can blow through 1.5°C in a single hot El Niño year
  (very likely before 2030) while the 20-year average that legally "counts"
  is still climbing toward it. Parallax shows the structural gap between the
  number the public watches and the number the treaty measures — and why the
  first 1.5°C headline year will not mean what everyone thinks it means. The
  Arctic angle sharpens it: the same report projects Arctic winters at 2.8°C
  above the 1991–2020 mean, **more than 3.5× the global anomaly** — the
  average hides a planet warming at wildly different speeds.
- **suggested kinds:** hero · prose · climate-spiral (monthly anomaly winding
  past the 1.5°C ring — single-month breaches vs the smooth 20-yr line;
  needs monthly global anomaly 2015–2026 from Copernicus/GISS) ·
  carbon-gauge (remaining budget arc reframing 1.5°C as a long-term mean, not
  a date; needs Global Carbon Budget remaining-budget figure) · data-readout
  (the 91% / 86% / 1.3–1.9°C / 20-year stats) · comparison (single-year
  breach vs 20-year sustained average, side by side) · analogy

## C-02 · The Forest Has a Dial, and It Isn't Temperature

- **status:** chosen
- **why now:** A 7 May 2026 *Nature* paper led by Nico Wunderling (Goethe
  University Frankfurt / Potsdam Institute) modelled the Amazon's tipping
  point with both deforestation and warming as inputs. The combination could
  flip more than two-thirds of the rainforest toward savanna **as early as
  the 2040s**. Carbon Brief carried it in the 8 May DeBriefed; Mongabay ran a
  full write-up the same week.
- **angle:** The climate conversation treats the Amazon as a thermometer
  problem — keep warming under 1.5°C and the forest survives. The paper
  inverts the controls. With continued clearing, the tipping point arrives at
  just **1.5–1.9°C** of warming once deforestation reaches **22–28%** (it is
  already at ~17–18%). But hold deforestation at today's level and the
  forest doesn't tip until **3.7–4°C**. Deforestation, not global
  temperature, is the dial humans are actually turning — and it is the one
  lever a single government (Brazil) can move in a single budget cycle. The
  mechanism is hydrological self-destruction: the trees recycle up to half
  their own rainfall, so clearing them dries the air for forest hundreds of
  kilometres downwind, propagating the collapse. Parallax shows the two
  threshold curves side by side and names the choice the framing usually
  hides.
- **suggested kinds:** hero · prose · comparison (the two tipping thresholds:
  with-deforestation 1.5–1.9°C @ 22–28% cleared vs climate-only 3.7–4°C;
  needs the paper's threshold pairs) · carbon-gauge or data-readout (17–18%
  cleared today vs the 22–28% band — distance to the dial's red zone) ·
  region-map (Amazon basin deforestation share by sub-region / arc of
  deforestation; needs MapBiomas or RAISG clearing data) · paradox (more rain
  recycling means clearing dries land that was never cut) · timeline
  (deforestation trajectory toward the band)
- **est. read time:** 7 min

## C-03 · Drink From a Fire Hose and You Still Go Thirsty

- **status:** open
- **why now:** A 13 May 2026 *Nature* paper by Justin Mankin (Dartmouth) and
  Corey Lesk (Université du Québec à Montréal) showed that when a region's
  annual rainfall concentrates into fewer, heavier storms — even with **total
  precipitation unchanged** — the land ends up drier. Inside Climate News
  covered it the same day, tying it to the American West staggering out of
  its worst winter on record as a possible El Niño looms.
- **angle:** The intuitive model is linear: more rain = wetter land, less rain
  = drier land. This paper breaks that. Holding the annual total fixed and
  just **concentrating** it into bigger bursts reduces how much water reaches
  soils, streams and aquifers, because downpours overwhelm soil capacity and
  pool on the surface to evaporate, while the long dry gaps between storms
  bake the ground. Mankin's line — *"if you're asking the land to drink from a
  fire hose, you're going to lose water"* — is the whole thesis. The two
  global hotspots for rising rainfall concentration since 1980 are the
  American West and the Amazon Basin (which ties straight back to C-02). The
  structural sting: water infrastructure and drought policy are sized to
  annual totals, so a basin can hit its rainfall "budget" on paper and still
  run dry — "potentially maladapted," in the authors' words. Parallax shows
  why the rain gauge lies.
- **suggested kinds:** hero · prose · analogy (the fire-hose vs the steady
  sip — soil as a vessel with a fill rate) · paradox (same total rainfall,
  drier land) · region-map (precipitation-concentration trend by region since
  1980, American West + Amazon flagged; needs the paper's concentration-index
  grid or a proxy) · data-readout (split of a heavy storm into runoff +
  evaporation vs soil recharge) · climate-strip (annual rainfall holding
  flat while a concentration index climbs — twin strips)
- **est. read time:** 6 min
- **sources (3-5):**

  - https://insideclimatenews.org/news/31032026/todays-climate-water-use-restrictions-snow-drought-western-us/
  - https://insideclimatenews.org/news/12052026/el-nino-climate-extremes-heatwaves-wildfires-floods/

  - https://climate.copernicus.eu/surface-air-temperature-april-2026
- **notes:** Primary source is a *Nature* paper (Mankin & Lesk, 13 May 2026);
  the ICN write-up is on the allowlist and carries the mechanism, the
  fire-hose quote, the two hotspot regions, and the "maladapted" framing —

  Niño developing during the season.
- **angle:** India treats the heat season and the monsoon as two separate news
  cycles — first the deaths, then the rain verdict. The IMD's own revision
  collapses them into one system: the same El Niño signal that is killing
  people in May (92% onset probability) is what is downgrading the rain that
  was supposed to end the dying in June. The structural truth is a coupled
  failure — the monsoon "core zone" of rain-fed agriculture is precisely the
  belt the IMD now flags for below-normal rain, so the heat doesn't just
  precede the drought, it shares a cause with it. Parallax shows the
  thermal-and-hydrological double bind as one mechanism, and the human cost

## C-05 · The Day 141 Countries Agreed Climate Harm Is a Wrong

- **status:** open
- **why now:** On 21 May 2026 the UN General Assembly adopted a resolution
  endorsing the International Court of Justice's 2025 advisory opinion that a
  state's failure to act on climate change is a "wrongful act" and that
  harmed nations may seek reparations. The vote was **141 for, 8 against, 28
  abstaining** — the US, Russia, Saudi Arabia, Iran, Israel, Belarus, Yemen
  and Liberia against. Grist and Carbon Brief both covered it (22 May).
- **angle:** Most coverage framed this as a feel-good show of unity. The
  structural story is the machinery underneath: a non-binding 2025 court
  *opinion* is being converted, vote by vote, into a *political instrument* —
  the resolution requests a UN Secretary-General progress report in 2027 and
  puts formal follow-up on the GA agenda in 2028, building a ratchet of
  accountability dates. And it shows who the architecture is built against:
  four Arab-state amendments that would have softened the obligation language
  and inserted 2°C / carbon-capture caveats were **defeated**, and the eight
  no-votes map almost exactly onto the world's largest oil exporters plus the
  US (which formally exited Paris in January 2026). Parallax reads the vote
  as a structure — the legal scaffolding of climate obligation being erected
  in real time, and the precise geography of who is trying to keep it from
  load-bearing.
