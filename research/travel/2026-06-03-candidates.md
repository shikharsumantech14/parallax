On **6 May 2026** Skift reported Japan's Tourism Agency is now drafting **national dual-pricing guidelines** (expected by March 2027), convening an expert panel to scale the Himeji model across the country's aging cultural sites.
- **angle:** This reads as "Japan charges tourists more," but the structural design is sharper and stranger than that. Himeji deliberately split the price on **residency, not nationality** — a foreign-born resident who pays local tax gets the local rate — which quietly reframes the question from "are you a tourist?" to "do you fund this place?" Parallax surfaces the real mechanism: this is a *congestion-and-maintenance* instrument disguised as a fairness measure. It cut footfall on a fragile wooden structure *and* raised the money to maintain it, hitting two policy targets that usually trade off against each other. The issue maps the arithmetic — who pays what, where the new money goes, what 17% fewer bodies does to a 17th-century timber keep — and asks why this two-tier model is spreading across Asia now while Western heritage sites still flinch at the idea.

- **angle:** The motel is treated as kitsch — neon, kidney pools, a nostalgia aesthetic. Parallax rebuilds it as **infrastructure**: the motel is the building that made car-first American travel physically possible, the unit that let the country sprawl along its highways before the Interstate, then the form that the Interstate nearly killed within a few decades of its birth. Its centennial coincides with Route 66's, and the two tell the same structural story from different angles — a freedom mythology built on a piece of standardised roadside real estate, distributed unevenly (the same era's Green Book existed because the open road wasn't open to everyone). The issue traces the form from the 1925 Milestone outward: how the motel templated the American journey, why it collapsed, and what the current restoration wave is actually preserving.
