## C-01 · The Passport Stamp Is Dead — and Nobody Told You

- **status:** open
- **why now:** Europe's Entry/Exit System (EES) went fully operational on April 10, 2026 — the first time in the Schengen Area's 30-year history that non-EU visitors are fingerprinted and face-scanned at every border crossing. The phased rollout (October 2025–April 2026) already caused 70% longer processing times and at least two airport systems crashing; ETIAS pre-authorisation follows later in 2026.
- **angle:** Most travelers understand EES as a minor bureaucratic upgrade. Parallax flips the frame: for 60+ visa-exempt countries, this is the end of anonymous movement through Europe. The system was designed not for tourism management but for tracking 90/180-day overstay violations — a rule that barely existed in practice is now enforced by biometric infrastructure. The issue shows how a migration-compliance system gets silently retooled into a mass travel checkpoint, and why the fingerprint you register in Paris in June will follow you to Athens in December.

The US inbound slump entered its ninth consecutive month of decline in January 2026 with overseas arrivals down 4.2%. The World Travel & Tourism Council warns proposed social-media screening could cost $15.7 billion in lost visitor spending and 157,000 jobs.
- **angle:** The conventional frame is "people are boycotting America." Parallax reframes it structurally: the US has been the world's largest travel market precisely because it built low-friction entry infrastructure over decades. The current shift isn't a boycott — it's an infrastructure regression. ## C-03 · The Price of Being Seen in Kyoto

- **status:** open
- **why now:** Kyoto's new five-tier accommodation tax took effect March 1, 2026, with the top rate of ¥10,000 (~$66) per person per night — a tenfold increase over the previous national high — targeting luxury stays above ¥100,000/night. The city explicitly cited overtourism as the rationale; projected annual tax revenue nearly doubles from ¥5.9 billion to ¥12.6 billion.
- **angle:** Parallax maps the architecture of the tax itself, not the "is this fair?" debate. The five-tier structure is a pricing signal disguised as a fiscal instrument: it taxes budget travelers lightly, charges mid-range visitors modestly, and levies a meaningful cost only on the ultra-luxury segment that contributes least to overtourism volume but most to property-price inflation. The issue asks: if you want fewer visitors, why is the ¥200 bracket still open? If you want more revenue from fewer visitors, why not price out everyone below ¥50,000? The answer reveals that Kyoto isn't managing tourism — it's managing political optics while funding infrastructure.
- **suggested kinds:** hero, data-readout, comparison, paradox, prose

## C-04 · The Flight That Wasn't Allowed to Exist

- **status:** open
- **why now:** On April 20, 2026, the FAA published a Federal Register order capping O'Hare International Airport at 2,708 daily operations from June 2 to October 24 — eliminating as many as 372 flights on peak days. United Airlines alone is cutting 100 flights per day. The FAA took this rare regulatory step citing air traffic control staffing shortfalls, ongoing construction, and airlines scheduling 15% more flights than the system could safely handle.
- **angle:** The headline is "flight cuts at O'Hare." The Parallax angle is structural: this is what happens when an airport's physical capacity (runways, gates) outpaces its invisible infrastructure (certified air traffic controllers, whose pipeline takes 5–7 years to fill). Airlines scheduled flights that technically fit on the schedule board but could not exist in the real world — and the FAA had to force them to stop. The issue maps the difference between airline slot capacity, physical airfield capacity, and ATC capacity, and shows how only the third one actually governs movement.
- **suggested kinds:** hero, timeline, data-readout, analogy, route-card

## C-05 · The Road That Turns 100 — and Still Doesn't Know What It Is

- **status:** open
- **why now:** Route 66 officially opened on November 11, 1926, making 2026 its centennial year. In May 2026, the Capitol Cruise world-record attempt (largest classic-car parade in history) is scheduled for May 30. The Threatt Filling Station — the only known Black-owned-and-operated gas station on Route 66 during the Jim Crow era — reopens in Luther, Oklahoma, this month as an interpretive center.
- **angle:** Route 66 is celebrated as American freedom mythology: open road, diners, neon, westward drift. Parallax examines what the road actually was: a federal infrastructure project that became the route of the Great Migration's Black travelers (who needed the Green Book to survive it), a conduit for Dust Bowl displacement, and then a highway superseded by its own Interstate successor within 40 years of opening. The centennial is a chance to ask what is being commemorated — and the Threatt Filling Station reopening provides the primary structural lens: a place that existed because the "freedom road" was not free for everyone.

World Heritage Day (April 18, 2026) prompted a wave of expert debate on whether India's ~3,000 ASI-protected monuments can be adaptively reused without violating conservation law.
- **angle:** India has roughly 3,000 monuments frozen under ASI protection — they can be visited but not functionally used, which is the opposite of how heritage conservation works in most of the world. The issue maps the gap between the legislation that governs ASI monuments and the global conservation charters that argue living use is the best form of preservation.
