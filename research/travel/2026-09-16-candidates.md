## C-01 · More than half of India's "international arrivals" are Indians coming home

- **status:** chosen
- **why now:** Skift's India Report ran "India's Domestic Tourism Boom May Be Its
Biggest Inbound Problem" on **14 September 2026**, two days ago, quoting a
tourism ministry official warning against "smugness". It lands on top of the
Ministry of Tourism's own portal numbers for 2025, published at
`data.tourism.gov.in` with month-wise data now running through **July 2026**:
foreign tourist arrivals **9.15 million, down 8.07%** on 2024 and roughly
**16% below the 10.93 million of 2019**, while domestic visits hit **4,287
million, up 45.55%**. Skift's earlier reporting (31 July 2026) has the global
promotion budget cut from about $47 million in 2015 to about $380,000, and the
73% collapse in arrivals from Bangladesh after India's August 2024 visa
restrictions.
- **angle:** Every story about this reaches for the same explanation: India has
stopped marketing itself, so foreigners stopped coming. The ministry's own
table says something stranger and more structural. India reports **20.22
million international tourist arrivals** for 2025 and only **9.15 million** of
them are foreign tourists. The other **11.07 million are non-resident
Indians**, and that number went **up 4.26%** in the same year the foreign
number fell 8%. So the headline arrivals figure India quotes at trade fairs is
majority-diaspora, and it has been for years. Underneath that sits a second
composition problem: the single largest "foreign tourist" source market was
Bangladesh, and most of those crossings were medical, family and trade travel,
not holidays. One bilateral visa decision in August 2024 removed roughly 1.3
million of them. The issue rebuilds the number from its parts and shows that
India's inbound "collapse" is two things wearing one label: a border closing,
and a diaspora being counted as tourists. The marketing budget is real, but it
is the third story, not the first.

## C-02 · Santorini and Mykonos charge the same €20. One lost ships, one gained them.

- **status:** open
- **why now:** The Greek peak-season cruise levy (**€20 per passenger at
Santorini and Mykonos, 1 June to 30 September**, dropping to €12 in shoulder
season and €4 in winter) has just finished its first full peak season, which
**ends on 30 September 2026**. The first-season read is the story: Santorini's
2026 scheduled cruise calls fell sharply while Mykonos, under the **identical
levy**, grew. In the same window **Cannes' ban on ships carrying more than
1,000 passengers took effect on 1 January 2026**, Amsterdam cut its annual
allowance to **100 ships** from 190, and Norway pushed its zero-emission fjord
requirement from 2026 out to **2032**.
- **angle:** Every port on the northern Mediterranean has now pulled a lever, and
the levers are not the same lever. Some cap the ship (Cannes: 1,000
passengers; Venice: 25,000 gross tonnes). Some cap the count (Barcelona: 7
ships a day, down from 10; Amsterdam: 100 a year; Villefranche: 65). Some
charge a head tax (Iceland, Greece). Some cap the bodies on shore (Santorini:
8,000 cruise passengers a day, a figure traced to a 2018 University of the
Aegean carrying-capacity study). Put Santorini and Mykonos side by side and the
experiment runs itself, because the price is held constant and only the cap
varies. The finding is that the price was never the lever. **€20 on a cruise
fare of a few thousand is well under one percent**, which no traveller feels,
while a hard daily cap enforced by a berth-allocation queue decides which ships
exist on which day. The issue shows a coastline where the visible instrument
(a fee, announced, resented, reported) does almost nothing, and the invisible
one (an allocation algorithm in a port office) does almost everything. There is
a second structural layer worth keeping: Santorini's 8,000 covers cruise
passengers only. Ferry and air arrivals are uncapped, so an island of roughly
15,000–25,000 residents still takes millions of visitors a year with the cap
fully in force.
- **suggested kinds (drawn graphics first):**
  - `city-compare` — *never published*. Exactly two places, winner per row:
  Santorini against Mykonos on levy, daily cap, scheduled calls 2025 vs 2026,
  annual passengers, resident population, passengers per resident. **Data:**
  levy tiers, the 8,000 cap, Santorini's 980,771 passengers on 592 vessels in
  2019 against about 1.3 million on 800 ships in 2023, resident population.
  **Source:** AFAR's Greek levy report (T4) plus the Greek port authority
  berth-allocation schedules (see notes).
  - `margin-bullets` [i] — *never published*. Eight ports, each against its
  own limit in its own unit: Cannes 1,000 passengers a ship and 6,000 ashore a
  day; Amsterdam 100 ships a year against 190 before; Barcelona 7 a day against
  10; Venice 25,000 gross tonnes; Villefranche 2,500 passengers and 65 ships a
  year; Santorini 8,000 a day; Iceland ISK 2,500 a head; Dubrovnik 2 ships at
  once. This kind exists for exactly this shape, measurements in mixed units
  each against its own requirement. **Data + source:** AFAR's port-by-port
  feature (T4), cross-checked against each municipality's decree.
  - `region-map` — *never published*. European coastal states shaded by the
  kind of lever each has chosen, so the patchwork reads as a map rather than a
  list. **Data:** the restriction type per country. **Source:** AFAR (T4) +
  Skift (T4).
  - `benchmark-chart` — the per-passenger charge as a share of a typical cruise
  fare, ports ranked, to show how small every one of them is. **Data:** fee per
  passenger, typical fare. **Source:** AFAR (T4).
  - `route-globe` [3D] — *never published*, travel signature. One western-Med
  itinerary arced across a globe with the calls that had to move. **Only if**
  the researcher can source real port coordinates and a real published 2026
  itinerary; otherwise drop it rather than invent the arc.

## C-03 · The United States hosted the World Cup and its visitor numbers still fell

- **status:** open
- **why now:** On **2 September 2026** travel executives met the president at
the White House and asked for a commitment to **more than 100 million
international visitors a year by 2030**, which would take the title of
most-visited country from France. Three weeks earlier, on **13 August 2026**,
the figures for the World Cup summer came in: overseas visits fell **1.8% in
June** to 2.8 million and **7% in July** to just over 3 million, a fourth
straight monthly decline and roughly **23% below pre-pandemic levels**, in the
two months the country was staging the largest sporting event it has ever
hosted. Inbound had already fallen **5.5% across 2025**, with Canadian travel,
the single largest source market, down close to **30%**.
- **angle:** A mega-event is supposed to be the one thing that can move a
national tourism number, and this is the cleanest natural experiment anyone
will get: 104 matches, millions of ticket-holders, and the arrivals line went
down anyway. The structural reading is that a country's inbound number is not
set by what it puts on and advertises, it is set by the friction at its
border and the price of getting through it, and those move on a different
clock entirely. A **$250 visa integrity fee**, social-media screening, longer
interview waits and a political climate all sit upstream of any campaign
downstream. So the target announced on 2 September and the machinery already
running are pulling on the same number in opposite directions. The issue holds
the two against each other: what a 100-million target requires per year in
growth, and what the last twenty months of monthly data actually did. It is
also an India story at the edges, because Indian travel to the US is among the
source markets that shrank while Indian outbound overall grew.
- **suggested kinds (drawn graphics first):**
  - `commit-grid` — *never published*. Every month since the slide began as
  one cell, shaded by its year-on-year change, so twenty months of near-solid
  one colour is the whole argument at a glance, with the World Cup months
  marked and indistinguishable. **Data:** monthly overseas arrivals, y/y change.
  **Source:** monthly inbound series as reported by Skift (T4), to be traced to
  the official series (see notes).
  - `approval-chart` — *never published*. Two series over time rather than
  approve/disapprove: world international arrivals indexed to 2019 against US
  arrivals indexed to 2019. The crossover in 2024–25, where the world clears
  its own pre-pandemic line and the US does not, is the finding. **Data:**
  annual global and US arrivals. **Source:** UN Tourism World Tourism Barometer
  (T0) + World Bank ST.INT.ARVL (T1).
  - `region-map` — *never published*. Source markets shaded by change in
  travel to the US, Canada as the deepest cell. **Data:** arrivals by country
  of residence. **Source:** see the sourcing flag in notes.
  - `benchmark-chart` — most-visited countries ranked on annual arrivals, the US
  against France and Spain, with the 100-million target drawn as a marked line
  the bar has to reach. **Data:** annual arrivals by country. **Source:** UN
  Tourism Barometer (T0) / World Bank (T1).
  - Cards, at most one each: `you-think` (a mega-event lifts arrivals),
  `number-sense` (what 100 million by 2030 needs every single year).
- **est. read time:** 6 min
