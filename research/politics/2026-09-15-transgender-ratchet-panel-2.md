## Findings on the twelve test points

**1. Card 04, the fairness finding. Closed. The misreading is no longer
available.** The card now reads: *"Abduction with mutilation or castration, to
force a transgender identity on the person abducted, is the offence here. The
punishment is ten years in prison, up to life, plus a fine from ₹2 lakh."*

"The person abducted" reads as the victim at scrolling speed, for all four
readers, and the reason is structural rather than stylistic: **the sentence
contains exactly one human noun phrase, and it is in the harmed role.** The
thing forced is an identity; the one it is forced on is a person; the doer is an
abductor the sentence does not have to name, because "abduction" carries them.
There is no noun in the sentence a skimming reader can put in the wrongdoer's
slot. Sana's first-pass error (binding "a transgender identity" to "ten years to
life") cannot recur, because the two phrases no longer sit adjacent and the
penalty now has "in prison" between it and anything else. All four readers
retold the card as a protection, and two used the word "protect" unprompted.

Two residuals, neither worth spending on. The offender is grammatically absent,
so the doer slot is filled by implicature rather than by a noun — I tested it
and every reader filled it with "whoever kidnapped them", not with anyone from
card 01. And "a fine from ₹2 lakh" asks the reader to read *from* as a minimum;
Sana read it as a flat amount. Neither changes who is punished or who is
protected.

**2. The hero's key. Closed, with one positional note.** The `plain` line now
ends *"A row with no filled cell means nobody holds it"*, and all four readers
used it. Meera and Karthik both retold the empty row in the key's own terms
without reaching for the caption, which is the change: in the first pass the
argument of the graphic sat on the caption's shoulders, and it now sits on the
figure's own key.

The honest qualifier: **the key renders below the grid**, so a top-down reader
still meets the empty row a beat before its definition. Sana reads the grid, has
a half-second of "is this missing data", and the next line answers her. That is
a re-read, which is why she scores 4 and not 5. It is a render-order fact, not a
wording fact, and no word in the twenty would change it.

**3. "Recommend to the magistrate". Nothing on the page invites "the board
examines the applicant" any more.** I checked every instance of *examine* and
every sentence in which the board appears: card 03 gives *examining* to the
magistrate and its object is the recommendation; the `jargon-buster` says
"recommends to the magistrate. It does not decide"; `three-steps` says "makes a
recommendation to the magistrate"; the matrix row now says "Recommend to the
magistrate"; the dek and section 1 both say *recommends*. Karthik's first-pass
complaint — the row contradicting the card — is gone, and he said so.

One residual, and it misroutes away from the forbidden reading rather than
towards it. Card 03's "after examining a designated medical board's
recommendation" puts the possessive after a noun phrase a fast eye has already
closed, so **Meera briefly read "examining a designated medical board"** — the
magistrate examining the board, not the board examining the applicant. She
corrects on the possessive. The record's verb is intact either way.

**4. The certificate has its object earlier. Confirmed.** Dek, then section 1's
"a district magistrate's certificate", then section 3's full gloss. The one
remaining friction is that the dek's "the certificate" is a definite article on
a first mention, which reads as a forward reference rather than a gap. No reader
stopped on it.

**5. `bill-passage`: eleven days. Confirmed, with a one-day residual.** The
seventeen-day arithmetic is gone: eyebrow and title now measure the same pair,
the caption pins both endpoints ("Introduced on 13 March, passed by voice vote
eleven days later"), and section 6's intro reinforces 24 March by placing the
committee "four days before the vote". Meera got eleven directly.

**Karthik got twelve.** The stack carries two votes (Lok Sabha 24 March, Rajya
Sabha 25 March), and "the vote", singular, can bind to the second. He went to
the caption and resolved it. The gap is one day against the seventeen it
replaced, and closing it means naming a House in a five-word title field.

**6. The section 7 caveat. Works for all four now, and the resignation statement
is out of its scope.** *"The lines spoken in the debate below reach you through
press reports, not Parliament's own transcript."* "Parliament's own transcript"
is a concrete object every reader can picture, which is exactly what "the
parliamentary record" was not; Aarav no longer reads it as hedging, because
"reach you" frames it as a service rather than a disclaimer. Two readers said,
unprompted, that they now knew where the words came from.

And **"spoken in the debate" excludes the written statement**, which the blanket
form caught. The followup's own verb, "wrote in a resignation statement", marks
it as outside the caveat's scope; Meera and Karthik both drew the line
correctly. The one place in the issue where the community speaks for itself is
no longer discounted by a caveat it never needed.

**7. NCTP. Confirmed.** "the National Council of Transgender Persons, the NCTP,
a government advisory body" arrives two sentences before the locked quotation
uses the acronym, with a role phrase attached. Karthik and Aarav both made the
join without a backtrack; Sana, who stopped here in the first pass, read through.

**8. Timeline. All four confirmed.** "it died when that Lok Sabha ended" binds
to "the Lok Sabha" one line above and cost no reader a stop, and it had an
unplanned benefit: **Meera's first-pass objection is gone.** She had re-read the
2016 event as a third bill contradicting the primer's "two laws"; the new note
tells her it cleared one House and never became law, which is exactly the
distinction she was missing. "gaps in the 2019 Act" names which law. "becomes
law" retires *assent* at the point where it mattered. And naming the Supreme
Court in the last event's label, with "Separately, the Rajasthan High Court" in
the note, ends the fusion — Meera and Sana both kept the two courts apart this
time.

One new snag in that note, and it is Sana's lost sentence: **"then deleted
**it**"**. The pronoun's nearest competitor is "the law". English prefers
publish X → delete X, and "included by mistake" cannot describe a statute, so
the reading is recoverable — but on a page about whether a law survives, "a High
Court deleted the law" is a wrong fact a reader should not be able to hold even
for a beat.

**9. The title. Worth the one word of head slack, and it is the best use of
it.** The garden path is real and repeatable: the first six words are a
well-formed question, so "moved" arrives with no subject marker and the clause
re-parses. Aarav and Sana both re-read. Against that: both recover in under a
second, the recovered reading *is* the finding, and the dek repairs it
immediately. It costs a beat on the one field that has to survive being seen
alone on a share card. The move is to stop the opening words resolving as a
complete question before the verb lands — not to change what the title claims.

**10. Tile 5. The one remaining fix I would rank first.** "Share with any
identity card · <5% · An activists' estimate, not a count." The label does say
which card (*any*), and the note does mark the figure's status. What it does not
say is **whose share** — and the tile sits fifth in a row that reads as a funnel
(counted → applied → issued → refused → …). A reader who carries the funnel into
the last tile lands on "fewer than 5% got the certificate", which is a different
claim from the one the tile makes, and it is precisely the certificate-coverage
ratio the storyboard forbids anyone from computing (§5, D6/D7). Meera and
Karthik read the label and stayed correct. Sana retold it as "hardly anyone has
a card", which is the right direction and the wrong denominator.

**11. Section 1's `note`. It floats, and it is the cheapest donor on the page.**
"Both laws are named for protection of rights. The name never moved." The page
never prints either law's name, so the observation cannot be checked by the
reader and no reader used it in a retell. It costs nobody a re-read — it simply
does no work. Printing a name to anchor it is barred (no name may be added), so
the choice is to leave it or to cut it and free eleven body words.

**12. "Assent". Fine as it stands, no word needed.** It survives only as the
fourth stage label, with "Signed into law, and in force the same day" directly
under it. All four readers took the label from the note. Aarav knows it from
newspaper headlines; Sana did not need to.

## Journalism check: does it inform without advocating?

**Yes, and more clearly than in the first pass.** The evidence is the retells,
not the prose:

- Every verb the readers gave back is procedural: *narrowed, deleted,
  recommends, issues or refuses, reported, died, challenged*. Not one reader
  used an evaluative word, because none was there to pick up.
- **The penalty card now reads as protection, which is what the record says it
  is.** In the first pass one reader briefly had transgender people on the wrong
  side of a criminal sentence. That is closed, and it was the single most
  important thing on this page to close.
- The minister's defence is quoted first and in full, before either critical
  voice, and all four readers retold it in that order. The critical lines are
  attributed to named speakers with their roles, and the whole block is now
  marked as press-reported without that marking spilling onto the community
  member's own written statement.
- Section 8's title states its numbers rather than a verdict; all four retold it
  as numbers.
- No reader came away with a claim about an effect on any person, because the
  issue asserts none. Asked what the law had done to anyone, all four answered
  with what it changed about the procedure — which is the whole editorial
  discipline of this rewrite, holding under test.

A transgender reader would recognise this account. The law's own vocabulary is
visibly quoted rather than adopted (card 01's "people the law names as…"), the
publication's own vocabulary for the same people is neutral and current
("trans-men, trans-women and genderqueer persons"), and the two never blur. The
one thing a reader from the community would notice is that the issue's three
critical voices outnumber the one supportive one, 2:1 — that ratio is the
reported record of the day, the section is titled "as reported", and the
government's position leads. It sits inside journalism, not advocacy.

## What would fix it (direction only, never the sentence)

Nothing here holds the issue. Ranked by what a reader loses, with what each
comes out of.

1. **Section 8, tile 5 — ~4 words, from the twenty of body slack.** The tile's
   fifth position in a funnel lets a reader supply the wrong denominator and
   manufacture the one ratio this issue refuses to compute. Name whose share it
   is. The note's own cap has four words free (6 of ≤ 10 used), so this fits
   without touching the label.
2. **Section 2, last event note — 1 word, from the same allowance.** Replace the
   pronoun in "then deleted it" with the noun it stands for. A reader should not
   be able to hold "a High Court deleted the law", even briefly, on this page.
3. **The title — the one word of head slack.** Stop the first six words
   resolving as a complete question before the verb arrives. The claim stays
   exactly as it is; "twice" is the argument and "in law" is load-bearing.
4. **Reserve, not a recommendation: section 1's `note` is eleven body words that
   no reader used.** If anything above needs more room than the twenty allow,
   that is where it comes from.

Fixes 1 and 2 together cost about five words, so all three fit inside the stated
budget without cutting the note.
