## C-01 · The Roll Is the Border Now

- **status:** open
- **why now:** On May 27, 2026 a Supreme Court bench led by CJI Surya Kant
  (with Justices Joymalya Bagchi and Vipul Pancholi) delivered a 124-page
  judgment upholding the Election Commission's Special Intensive Revision
  (SIR) of electoral rolls as constitutionally valid — rejecting the
  "ultra vires" challenge and affirming the EC's power under Articles 324–327
  and the Representation of the People Act, 1950. The same judgment directs
  the EC to forward to the **Union Government**, within four weeks, the names
  of people deleted from the rolls over "doubtful citizenship." The SIR,
  which began in Bihar in July 2025 and now runs across 12 states, had
  already removed ~91 lakh names (11.88% of a 7.66-crore electorate) in
  West Bengal before its April 2026 vote.
- **angle:** The earlier coverage framed SIR as a partisan electoral-rolls
  fight. The verdict reframes it structurally: the Court has effectively
  fused two registers that Indian law kept separate — the **electoral roll**
  (who may vote) and **citizenship** (who belongs). By blessing a process
  that routes "doubtful" deletions to the Union Government for citizenship
  examination, the judgment turns a routine voter-list cleanup into the
  intake funnel for a citizenship determination the voter never asked for,
  with the burden of proof on the individual. Parallax traces how a
  procedural revision became a constitutional instrument: the 1995 precedent
  the Court reinterpreted, the four legal questions the bench answered, and
  why "you were on the roll" no longer guarantees you stay on it. Not a
  conspiracy read — a forensic map of where an administrative process now
  hands off to the machinery of belonging.
- **suggested kinds:** hero, timeline, bill-passage, vote-flow, data-readout,
  paradox, power-matrix, quote, prose
  - *vote-flow* (Sankey): the SIR pipeline as flow — pre-revision electorate →
    retained / deleted, and deleted → restored-on-appeal / referred-to-Union-
    Govt for citizenship. Needs the 91-lakh deletion + appeal-restoration
    counts (ECI / Wire reporting).
  - *bill-passage* repurposed as a **process-stage track** for the SIR's path
    through the courts (Bihar SIR → interim refusal to stay → final verdict →
    four-week citizenship-referral directive). Flag for the drafter: if the
    stage metaphor reads as legislative when it's judicial, fall back to
    *timeline*.
  - *power-matrix*: institution × actor control grid (EC / Supreme Court /
    Union Govt / voter) showing who holds the burden at each stage.
  - *data-readout* for the headline figures (91,02,577 deletions; 11.88%;
    7.66 cr; 12 states; 124-page judgment; 4-week directive).

## C-02 · The Deputy Always Collects

- **status:** open
- **why now:** On June 3, 2026 — today — D.K. Shivakumar is being sworn in
  as Karnataka's Chief Minister at Lok Bhavan, Bengaluru, ending a power
  struggle that ran since the 2023 election. Siddaramaiah, Karnataka's
  longest-serving CM, resigned on May 28 after the Congress high command
  "directed" him to honour a reported rotation arrangement under which
  Shivakumar — who delivered the 2023 win as state party chief and served
  2.5 years as Deputy CM — would take over at the half-term mark. The
  half-term point had passed in November 2025; the CLP unanimously elected
  Shivakumar leader on May 30. Siddaramaiah declined a Rajya Sabha exit and
  was named to the Congress Working Committee.
- **angle:** The conventional frame is "Congress drama, resolved." The
  Parallax frame is structural: the **two-power-centre, rotation-promise
  state government** has become Congress's signature design — and its
  signature failure mode. A party that lacks a single dominant national
  leader manages its strongest state leaders by pairing a CM against a Deputy
  CM / state-president rival and floating an unwritten rotation deal that the
  high command can invoke or deny at will. Karnataka 2023 → 2026 is the
  clean case; the same architecture produced the Gehlot–Pilot standoff in
  Rajasthan and the Baghel–Singh Deo tension in Chhattisgarh, both of which
  ended in electoral defeat. The issue maps the mechanism — why the design
  exists (it buys a coalition of egos into one ticket), why it recurs (no
  central arbiter with independent legitimacy), and what it costs (years of
  governance spent on succession rather than delivery). The deputy always
  collects, eventually, but the bill is paid in stalled government.
- **suggested kinds:** hero, timeline, swing-dial, power-matrix, comparison,

  - *power-matrix*: rival-camp control of state institutions (CMO, KPCC
    president, cabinet share, high-command favour) before vs after the switch.
  - *comparison*: Karnataka rotation vs the Rajasthan (Gehlot–Pilot) and
    Chhattisgarh (Baghel–Singh Deo) two-centre standoffs — same design,

  - *timeline*: May 2023 win → Deputy-CM bargain → Nov 2025 half-term → six-hour

## C-03 · The Report Due in July That Could Resync Every Election

- **status:** open
- **why now:** On March 18, 2026 the Lok Sabha extended the tenure of the
  39-member Joint Parliamentary Committee (chaired by BJP MP P.P. Chaudhary)
  examining the Constitution (129th Amendment) Bill, 2024 and the Union
  Territories Laws (Amendment) Bill — the "One Nation, One Election" package —
  pushing its report deadline to the first day of the last week of the
  **2026 Monsoon Session** (Parliament sits July 21–Aug 12). The JPC has
  publicly floated that simultaneous polls could save ~Rs 7 lakh crore and
  add up to 1.6% to GDP. This lands directly after the April 2026 collapse of
  the government's delimitation amendment (which fell 54 votes short of the
  two-thirds bar) — meaning the same supermajority arithmetic that killed
  delimitation now governs ONOE.
- **angle:** The conventional frame is "cost savings vs federalism," argued
  as opinion. The Parallax frame is the **arithmetic of constitutional
  rewiring**: ONOE is not one decision but a chain of constitutional
  amendments (Articles 83, 172, 356, plus a new 82A) that must clear a
  two-thirds majority in both Houses, and — for the provisions touching
  state legislative powers — ratification by half the state assemblies under
  Article 368(2). The government's own delimitation defeat in April proved it
  cannot reach two-thirds when regional parties whose states stand to lose
  vote as a bloc. The issue maps the actual mechanism: which articles change,
  which require state ratification (the HLC report claims some do not — a
  contested reading), how the "sync once, then dissolve assemblies with the
  Lok Sabha" clause shortens elected state terms, and why the JPC report
  timing matters. The structural revelation: ONOE's real obstacle is not the
  Opposition's "Modi phobia" — it is the Constitution's own counter-majoritarian
  ratification design, the same design that just defeated the government once.

  power-matrix, comparison, timeline, prose
  - *bill-breakdown*: clause-by-clause of the 129th Amendment — Article 82A

    gate marked as the hard one. (Genuine fit: the kind exists for exactly
    this.)

## C-04 · Twenty-One States, Three Billion Dollars

- **status:** open
- **why now:** On June 2, 2026 Ashutosh Varshney (Brown University) published
  a column in ThePrint naming the contradiction directly: after the 2026
  assembly results reversed its 2024 Lok Sabha setback, the BJP now governs
  21 of 28 states alone or in coalition — roughly 75% of India's population,
  with only 7 states under opposition control — even as the investment economy
  hollows out. Net FDI for 2025–26 was just **$3 billion** ($74bn in, $71bn
  out); the private investment rate slipped from 34.3% of GDP (2014) to 32.9%
  (2024); corporate investment fell from ~17% of GDP (2007) to roughly half
  that by 2025; headline growth of 6–7% rests largely on debt-financed public
  capex.
- **angle:** The conventional frame treats political strength and economic
  performance as a single scoreboard — a strong government must be a competent
  manager. The Parallax frame separates them and asks why they have
  **decoupled**: India has built the most electorally dominant central
  government in a generation while private capital quietly exits the room.
  The structural argument is that political dominance and private-investment
  confidence are not the same signal, and may even be inversely related when
  dominance is achieved through centralisation, unpredictability (retrospective
  rule changes, agency-led enforcement), and a shrinking space for the
  institutional guarantees capital actually prices — independent courts,
  predictable federalism, contract certainty. Parallax maps the gap: the
  electoral-map ascent (21 states) beside the capital-flight readout ($3bn
  net FDI), and asks what an economy is buying when it trades institutional
  predictability for a strong centre. A data-led structural essay, not a
  partisan scorecard.
- **suggested kinds:** hero, data-readout, power-matrix, approval-chart,
  comparison, paradox, prose
  - *power-matrix*: the 21-of-28-states control grid (BJP-alone / NDA-coalition /
    opposition) as an institution × bloc readout — the political-dominance
    half of the paradox.
  - *data-readout*: the investment collapse figures ($3bn net FDI; 34.3%→32.9%
    private investment; 17%→~8.5% corporate investment; 6–7% growth) — the
    economic half.
  - *approval-chart* or *comparison*: dominance-index vs investment-rate over
    time, the two lines diverging — the visual core of the paradox.
  - *paradox*: the explicit "dominant government / departing capital" frame

## C-05 · The Count That Skipped Four Generations

- **status:** open
- **why now:** India's 16th Census — the first since 1931 to enumerate caste,
  and billed as the world's first fully **digital** census — is now physically
  underway. The House Listing & Housing phase runs April 1 – September 30,
  2026 (states began on staggered dates: Gujarat's self-enumeration opened
  May 17, house-listing June 1); population enumeration with caste follows in
  February 2027. On the legal side, the Supreme Court in May 2026 dismissed a
  petition seeking to exclude caste enumeration, clearing the last challenge.
  The Cabinet has sanctioned Rs 11,718 crore. For the first time, households
  can self-enumerate via a mobile app in a 15-day window before enumerators
  arrive.
- **angle:** The conventional frame is "caste census = reservation politics."
  The Parallax frame is the **data void itself**: India's entire architecture
  of caste-based reservation — the 27% OBC quota, the 50% cap, every "creamy
  layer" and sub-quota dispute — has been litigated and legislated for 75
  years on top of a population number last actually counted in **1931**, then
  carried forward through the 1980 Mandal Commission's estimates. The 2027
  census is not merely a policy input; it is the first moment the Indian state
  will *know* the size of the population it has been governing by proxy. The
  issue traces the 96-year absence and its downstream effects — and adds a
  second structural layer the older framing missed: the **method** is now the
  story too. A digital, self-enumerated, app-based count changes who gets
  counted and how errors propagate (connectivity gaps, self-reporting of
  caste, the data-security surface of a 140-crore digital register). Parallax
  maps both the void being filled and the new instrument filling it.
- **suggested kinds:** hero, timeline, data-readout, analogy, paradox,
  comparison, prose
  - *timeline*: 1931 (last caste count) → 1951 (caste dropped) → 1980 Mandal
    estimate → 1992 Indra Sawhney 50% cap → 2027 first recount — the 96-year
    rail.
  - *data-readout*: the headline figures (1931; Rs 11,718 cr; April 1–Sep 30
    2026 house-listing; Feb 2027 enumeration; 15-day self-enumeration window;
    ~140 cr population; first digital census).
  - *analogy*: governing a 27% quota on a 1931 number = steering by a map
    last surveyed before independence.
  - *comparison*: the paper-census method (1931–2011) vs the 2027 digital /
    self-enumeration method — what each can and cannot see.
- **est. read time:** 6 min
