# Reader panel: Who decides your gender in law moved twice

- **Draft:** src/content/issues/2026-05-02-transgender-ratchet/index.mdx
- **Storyboard:** research/politics/2026-09-15-transgender-ratchet-storyboard.md
- **Pass:** first (after draft)
- **Read:** 2026-09-15
- **Verdict:** REVISE

All twelve quiz cells come back correct. The issue teaches its argument: every
reader can say who decides now and that it moved twice. What it loses them on
is local and mechanical — one object named four sections after it is first
used, one verb assigned to the wrong actor in the hero, two terms of art left
unglossed in the timeline, and one acronym dropped inside the longest sentence
on the page. Nothing here is structural.

## The hook

**It stopped every thumb.** "In 2014 the Supreme Court settled it: you decide
your own gender. By March 2026, 5,566 applications had been refused, with
nowhere to appeal." — the "you", the number and the reversal are all there, and
Sana read to the end of the second sentence, which is the whole test.

What the head lacks is not in the hook. It is that **the issue's central
object is never named above the fold**. "Applications" (hook), "issues" (dek)
and "a certificate" (section 1) all arrive before section 3 says what a
certificate of identity is. Three readers reached for the missing noun.

- **Title** — the weakest field. "Who decides your gender in law moved *twice*"
  is a noun clause used as a subject, and on a phone the first six words read
  as a question. Aarav and Sana both re-read it. The dek and hook recover the
  meaning immediately, so it costs a beat, not the argument.
- **Dek** — "a district magistrate issues." ends on a transitive verb with
  nothing after it. Karthik read it as a truncation.
- **Primer** — clean. Three sentences, no snags, for all four readers.

## Findings on the seven test points

**1. The power moved twice — does a cold reader come away able to say who
decides now?** **Yes, all four.** The claim is carried in five places (title,
dek, section 1's `actually.text`, section 1's caption, the timeline's second
annotation) and no reader finished thinking the law merely "got stricter". The
line that secures it is the `jargon-buster` gloss "It does not decide" — every
reader used the word *recommends* for the board and *issues or refuses* for the
magistrate, unprompted. This is the issue's strongest result.

One asymmetry worth naming: the **2019 step is thinner than the other two**.
2014 and 2026 each get a flat declarative sentence; 2019 is assembled by the
reader from timeline event 3 ("Recognition now needs a certificate") and card
02 ("Section 4(2) of the earlier law recognised a right…"). Aarav and Sana both
retell 2019 as "you needed a certificate" and neither mentions that the right
itself survived on paper until 2026. That does not damage the answer to Q1, but
it is the only joint in the argument the reader makes themselves.

**2. The empty bottom row — read as "no appeal", or as missing data?** **Read
as "no appeal", by all four.** Not a BLOCK. Three things carry it: the intro
one line above ("Those refusals had nowhere to go"), the row label itself
("Appeal a refusal", which is a thing, not a blank), and the caption directly
below ("Neither law gives an appeal"). Every other row has exactly one filled
cell, so a row with none reads as *nobody*, not as *unknown*.

But it is carried by prose on both sides of the graphic, **not by the graphic's
own reading instructions.** The authored `plain` says what a filled cell means
and stops there: *"A filled cell is whoever holds that decision."* A reader who
uses the plain line as the key to the grid is told nothing about an empty one
and has to infer it. Sana got there via the caption, one beat late. That
`plain` line is about 120 of its 220 characters, so the room to close the
inference is already in the field. This is the single highest-value fix in the
issue.

**3. `bill-passage` — does "Introduced / PASSED" read as a same-day vote?**
**No.** The two dates differ on the face of the cards (13 Mar, 24 Mar) and the
caption states the interval outright. No reader retold it as a vote on
introduction day. The `plain` line's framing ("whether the bill cleared that
stage") holds for Meera, Sana and Karthik; Aarav looked at the first badge
twice before moving on, which is what took the section to 3 for him.

The section's actual problem is a different one, and it is a label, not the
component: **the eyebrow says ELEVEN DAYS over a card stack that runs 13 March
to 30 March, under a title that says "From introduction to *assent*".** Both
numerate readers did the arithmetic, got seventeen, and had to go to the
caption to learn that eleven is introduction-to-vote. The eyebrow and the title
currently describe two different intervals.

**4. The statutory vocabulary on card 01.** **The framing holds for all four.**
"people the law names as…" sits in the same sentence as the list, five words
ahead of it, and every reader attributed the vocabulary to the statute rather
than to Parallax. Nobody read the issue as using the word in its own voice.
Karthik, the most literal reader, retold the card as "the socio-cultural
communities the statute lists" — the framing did exactly its job.

Two observations to hold rather than act on. First, **it holds by a five-word
margin**: the attribution and the list are one sentence, and any trim that
separates them breaks the only protection the card has. Second, the issue's
*own* voice for the same people is "trans-men, trans-women and genderqueer
persons" (card 01's bullet), which is the community's vocabulary, used
neutrally, and it is the phrase the readers repeated back. The separation
between the statute's words and the publication's words is visible on the page.

**5. The quote section's reframing — careful sourcing, or hedging?** **Split.**
Meera and Karthik read "as reported by The Print" as careful sourcing and
respected it. Aarav and Sana cannot use it, because **"the parliamentary
record" is an unglossed term**: without knowing what the record is, a reader
cannot tell whether "not the parliamentary record" means "reported by a
journalist who was in the House" or "we could not find this anywhere official".
Aarav's reading tilted to the second. The sentence spends a reader's attention
without buying comprehension.

One fairness consequence, in my lane because it changes what the reader
believes about the evidence: **the caveat is written for one sentence and lands
on three.** It is calibrated to the minister's disputed rendering, but
"all three lines below" also covers Kalki Subramanium's resignation statement,
which is a written statement and was never a parliamentary-record item in the
first place. The blanket form quietly discounts the one place in the issue
where the community speaks for itself. Direction: the caveat's scope is the
thing to look at, not its presence.

**6. The two dropped claims — does the argument still hold?** **Yes.** No
reader needed the four-Article formulation or the surgery-precondition holding
to answer Q1, and none of them noticed an absence, because they never knew the
claims existed. What the reader gets of the 2014 judgment is "the Supreme Court
holds that your own word decides", plus the case name in the note, plus the
annotation "The right the 2026 amendment deletes begins here." That is a
complete, retellable answer to "what did 2014 establish".

The one thing the cut costs is **force, not comprehension**: the timeline's
2019 note ("A male or female certificate also required proof of surgery") now
lands as a neutral administrative detail rather than as a reversal, because the
page never says 2014 had anything to say about surgery. Meera read straight
past it. That is the honest price of not asserting what cannot be sourced, and
it is the right price to pay.

**7. The 40-word sentence.** Found. It is the closing sentence of the `quote`
section's `followup`:

> One of them, Kalki Subramanium, wrote in a resignation statement: "The
> decision to move this Bill forward without any formal consultation with
> myself or other community representatives of the NCTP undermines the very
> purpose for which this Council was established."

**It reads long on a phone — and length is not what costs the reader.** Thirty
of the forty words are a fixed verbatim quotation the storyboard locks, so the
word count is mostly not the writer's to spend. What loses Aarav and Sana is
**"NCTP", which is never expanded**. The body is named two sentences earlier
("the National Council of Transgender Persons, a government advisory body"),
so the join is available, but it is an extra inference asked at word 30 of the
longest sentence in the issue, at the end of a three-quote block, in the
section where attention is already lowest. Karthik made the join; Sana stopped.

## Journalism check: does it inform without advocating?

**Yes, and a transgender reader would recognise the account.** The evidence
from the retells, not from the prose:

- Every verb the readers repeated back is procedural — *narrowed, deleted,
  recommends, issues or refuses, reported*. Not one reader retold a section
  using an evaluative word, which means none was there to pick up.
- The minister's defence is quoted first, at full length, before any criticism,
  and all three readers who reached section 7 retold it in that order.
- Section 8's title states its own numbers rather than a verdict, and all four
  readers retold it as numbers.
- No reader came away with a claim about an effect on any person, because the
  issue asserts none.

**One line I would not ship as it stands**, and it is a comprehension defect
before it is anything else. Card 04:

> "Abduction with mutilation or castration to force a transgender identity
> brings ten years to life, plus a fine from ₹2 lakh."

This is the only card whose subject is a harm done *to* transgender persons,
and it never names the victim, never names the protected party, and never says
the word prison or jail ("ten years to life" *of what* — Karthik asked it
outright). At scrolling speed the two nearest noun phrases are "a transgender
identity" and "ten years to life", and Sana's first pass bound them together.
She corrected herself on the second pass from the card title ("New penalties"),
but on this subject that is the one misreading the page cannot afford to leave
available. Direction: the card needs its victim named; the penalty needs its
unit.

## What would fix it (direction only, never the sentence)

1. **Section 9, the hero's `plain` line:** it defines a filled cell and stops.
   Define the empty one in the same line. About 100 characters of the 220 are
   unused, and this is what would take the argument of the graphic off the
   caption's shoulders and onto the graphic's own key.
2. **Section 9, matrix row 2:** the row label gives *examine* to the medical
   board, while card 03, the `jargon-buster` and `three-steps` all give
   *examining* to the magistrate and *recommend* to the board. It is the one
   place on the page that invites "the board examines you" — the exact reading
   the editor notes forbid. The row's verb, not the cell, is what to change.
3. **The head and section 1:** the certificate is used three times ("applications",
   "issues", "a certificate") before section 3 names it. The gloss already
   exists and is excellent; it just arrives fourth. Either the object gets its
   noun once in the head, or section 1 carries it. The dek's "a district
   magistrate issues." is the sharpest instance, because the verb has no object
   at all.
4. **Section 4, card 04:** name who is being protected, and name the unit of
   the penalty. This is the fairness-critical one.
5. **Section 2, timeline:** two terms of art are unglossed on a desk where
   nothing else is. "lapsed" (event 2) and "assent" (event 6), plus "the gaps"
   (event 5), which names a thing the reader has never been told exists.
6. **Section 2, event 6:** "gets assent, and is challenged" plus a note about a
   *different* court reads as one fact. Meera and Sana both fused the challenge
   with the Rajasthan High Court. The connective between the two is missing.
7. **Section 6:** the eyebrow ELEVEN DAYS and the title "From introduction to
   *assent*" measure two different intervals over the same four cards. Pick the
   one the reader is meant to count.
8. **Section 7:** "the parliamentary record" needs its gloss in the same
   sentence, and "NCTP" needs to arrive expanded or not at all. Also worth a
   look: the caveat says "all three lines" when it is calibrated to one.
9. **Section 8, tile 5:** "Share with any identity card" does not say which card
   or whose share. Every other tile on the page says exactly what it counts.
10. **The title:** "Who decides your gender in law moved *twice*" garden-paths
    on a phone — the first six words read as a question. The finding is right
    and the word "twice" is the whole argument; it is the grammar of the subject
    clause that costs the beat.
