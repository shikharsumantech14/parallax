## C-01 · Eight judges said states could tax the mines. Parliament picked the one who said no.

- **status:** open
- **why now:** The Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation)
Amendment Bill, 2026 was introduced in the Lok Sabha on 10 August 2026,
passed the Lok Sabha on 12 August and the Rajya Sabha on 13 August. It
bars states from levying any tax or cess on mineral rights and
mineral-bearing land except as the Centre permits — reversing
*Mineral Area Development Authority v. Steel Authority of India*
(25 July 2024), where a nine-judge bench held 8:1 that the power was
the states'. Odisha's opposition parties are protesting the amended law.
- **angle:** Most readers will file this as "Centre versus states, again."
The structure underneath is stranger and more specific. The
bill does not argue with the majority; it writes the **lone dissent**
into statute. Justice B.V. Nagarathna's minority view — that royalty is
*itself* a tax fixed by Parliament, so a state levy on top is double
taxation — is the reasoning the 2026 Act adopts. And its money clause
runs in one direction only: state levies **not yet collected** are
deemed invalid, while anything **already deposited or recovered** is
not refundable. So the state that enforced its dues keeps the money and
the company that delayed payment owes nothing. A retrospective law that
rewards whoever waited. That is the Parallax shift: not the politics of
federalism but the arithmetic of who was paying attention to a court
deadline, and the ₹1.5 lakh crore that turns on it.

## C-02 · One in three Delhi voters is off the draft roll. In 68 of 70 seats, that is more than the winning margin.

- **status:** open
- **why now:** Delhi's SIR draft roll removed 47.56 lakh of 1.45 crore
voters — 32.78%, the highest rate in the country. A Sabar Institute
study found the deletions exceed the 2025 assembly winning margin in
68 of Delhi's 70 seats. The claims-and-objections window closes
**30 September 2026**; appeals are heard by 29 October and the final
roll publishes 4 November. Nationwide, over 13 crore names have gone
from draft rolls across 30 states and union territories since the
revision began in June 2025.
- **angle:** The reported story is a fight about whether the deletions
are honest. Parallax should not re-litigate that — the Supreme Court
settled the legality on 27 May 2026 and the intent is not knowable from
the data. The structural finding is narrower and much harder to argue
with: **the deletions are not distributed like errors.** They cluster
in states that voted in the last three years, and within Delhi they
land at a scale that is arithmetically larger than the margins that
decided those very seats. Whatever removed those names, the roll that
results cannot be checked against the election it follows. The issue is
about **what a number has to be before it stops being a correction**,
and about the one asymmetry nobody draws: a deletion is automatic,
while restoring a name takes a form, a hearing and a deadline of
30 September.

## C-03 · Parliament passed 11 of 12 bills while the Lok Sabha sat for 15% of its time

- **status:** chosen
- **why now:** PRS's vital statistics for the Monsoon Session 2026
(20 July – 13 August, 19 sitting days) record the Lok Sabha functioning
for **15%** of scheduled time and the Rajya Sabha for **33%**; Question
Hour ran at **1%** in the Lok Sabha and 12% in the Rajya Sabha. Twelve
bills were introduced and eleven passed, several "without any members
participating in the discussion." The session adjourned *sine die* on
13 August and, as of 14 September 2026, had still not been prorogued —
32 days, against a Rajya Sabha manual norm of two to ten.
- **angle:** The familiar frame is decline: Parliament is broken, nothing
gets done. The data says the opposite and that is the story.
**Output is intact. Examination is what collapsed.** Eleven of twelve
bills passed — a throughput most legislatures would envy — produced by
a chamber that was functioning one hour in seven. These are not in
tension; the second is what makes the first possible. Add the long
series: committees scrutinised 71% of bills in the 14th Lok Sabha and
60% in the 15th, 25% in the 16th, and about 13% since 2019. The scrutiny
machinery was not overwhelmed, it was routed around. Then the session
that will not formally end, because an un-prorogued House can be
reconvened with its pending business still alive. The Parallax shift is
that **the calendar is the instrument** — not the vote.
