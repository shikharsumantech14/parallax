## C-01 · India is not selling compute. It is selling power, land and water on a 20-year clock

- **status:** open
- **why now:** India's tracked data-centre power capacity passes **2 GW during
2026**, roughly double where it sat a year earlier, and the commitments
stacked on top of it are an order of magnitude larger: AirTrunk's **5 GW /
$30bn** (5 June 2026), Adani's **$100bn / up to 5 GW**, Reliance's **$110bn**
Jamnagar build, OpenAI–Tata's 100 MW with a 1 GW target. The lever underneath
them is fiscal, not technical: in **February 2026** Delhi offered foreign
cloud providers a **20-year tax holiday through 2047** on services sold
overseas if the workload runs from an Indian data centre. On **24 August
2026** the US Congressional Research Service put a number on the resource
side of the same trade — US data centres used **~17 billion gallons of water
in 2023, triple 2014** — with **over 80%** of it consumed indirectly, at the
power station rather than the campus.
- **angle:** The conventional framing is a race: India competing for AI
infrastructure. Parallax inverts the direction of the sale. A data centre
exporting tax-free compute to foreign customers imports almost nothing India
is short of and consumes several things it is very short of — firm power,
land under paddy, and water, most of it burned invisibly at the generating
station rather than at the shiny campus. Andhra Pradesh's package for
Google's $15bn Visakhapatnam project is **₹22,002 crore (about $2.4bn) over
20 years** in exemptions, land, discounted tariffs and water/power
reimbursement — a published, per-project price the state pays. Set that
against Epoch's satellite-measured build curve and the asymmetry is
structural, not rhetorical: the grid and water commitment is made in year
one, the tax revenue is waived until 2047, and a gigawatt campus employs a
few hundred people. The issue's job is to put a rupee figure on the
subsidy per megawatt and let the reader do the division.

## C-02 · Open models are four months behind, not four years. That is a waiting time, not a gap

- **status:** chosen
- **why now:** Epoch AI's capability tracking finds that **since January 2026
the best open-weight models have lagged frontier closed models by an average
of four months — 8 ECI points**, which Epoch itself calibrates as "the
difference between GPT-5 and GPT-5.5." The measured window runs Jan–29 May
2026 (closed: GPT-5.5 Pro 159.35, Gemini 3.5 Flash 156.31, Claude Opus 4.7
156.18; open: Kimi K2.5 148.22, GLM-5 146.62, DeepSeek-V3.2 146.46). Since
then the gap has kept compressing: Nathan Lambert calls **Kimi K3** the
moment "frontier open-weight models are now real," and Stanford HAI's **AI
Index 2026** technical chapter documents the same convergence.
- **angle:** The public argument about open models is framed as a capability
question — can they catch up, will they ever be as good. The measurement says
the question is already settled and the right unit is *time*. If the lag is a
roughly constant four months rather than a widening chasm, then frontier
capability is not a moat, it is a subscription you can decline: any
organisation willing to run four months behind gets today's frontier for the
cost of the GPUs, with no per-token bill and no vendor. That reframes the
whole Indian deployment calculus — and it is the exact inverse of the
economics Parallax published in the Uber token-bill issue, which is the
follow-on, not a retelling. The second structural point: a constant lag is
itself evidence about *how* capability transfers, and the release lineage
shows the mechanism.
- **suggested kinds (drawn graphics first):**
  - `elo-river` *(never published)* — the braid. **Data needed:** 6–10
  models' dated capability ratings, open and closed, as two coloured families
  from Jan 2026 to Sept 2026; the crossovers are the story. **Source:** Epoch
  AI ECI series (model, release date, ECI, open/closed flag) with LMArena as
  the cross-check.
  - `version-graph` *(never published)* — the release lineage. **Data
  needed:** open-weight model releases as a branch/merge/tag graph — Llama,
  Qwen, DeepSeek, GLM, Kimi — with dates and derivative counts. **Source:**
  Hugging Face model hub (T1, per-artifact metadata + download signals).
  - `scaling-plot` — training compute against capability, log axis.
  **Data needed:** training FLOP and ECI per model for the same set; Epoch's
  table carries both. **Source:** Epoch AI (T1). *(Already published — the
  two never-published kinds above carry the floor.)*
  - Supporting, non-drawn: `jargon-buster` for ECI and "open weights" (which
  is not open source, and the distinction is load-bearing here).

## C-03 · The EU's AI law has been in force since 2024. Only last month could it fine anyone

- **status:** open
- **why now:** On **2 August 2026** the European Commission's supervision and
enforcement powers over general-purpose AI model providers came into force —
the power to demand documentation, run evaluations, order recalls and
mitigations, and levy fines of up to **3% of worldwide annual turnover or
€15 million, whichever is higher** (Article 101). The same date switched on
Article 50's transparency duties for synthetic audio, image, video and text.
The obligations themselves had applied since 2 August 2025; providers got a
one-year grace period before anyone could act on them, and **models released
before August 2025 have until 2 August 2027**. Over 20 providers had signed
the voluntary Code of Practice by August 2026.
- **angle:** Everyone reads a law as a switch — passed, therefore binding. The
AI Act is built instead as a **staircase with at least thirteen dated
steps**, running 2 February 2025 to 31 December 2030, and the steps are
sequenced so that the duty always arrives before the enforcement, and the
enforcement always arrives before the standard it is meant to enforce. The
structural reveal: compliance is *voluntary by construction* for the
interval, because the harmonised European standards that would define
conformity do not exist yet, so adherence to a Code of Practice is the only
practical way to demonstrate it. That is not a loophole someone found — it is
the architecture, written into Article 113. An Indian reader's stake is
direct: this is the template every other jurisdiction is now copying or
refusing, and India has taken the other road entirely.
- **suggested kinds (drawn graphics first):**
  - `state-timeline` *(never published, instrument)* — the staircase.
  **Data needed:** one lane per obligation class (prohibitions + AI literacy,
  GPAI obligations, Commission enforcement powers, Article 50 transparency,
  high-risk Annex III, legacy GPAI, sandboxes), each switching state on its
  dated milestone from 2 Feb 2025 to 31 Dec 2030. **Source:**
  artificialintelligenceact.eu Implementation Timeline (T0) — all thirteen
  dates and their articles are published there; EUR-Lex for the Article 113
  text itself.
  - `arch-stack` *(never published)* — the risk pyramid as layers.
  **Data needed:** the four risk tiers (prohibited / high-risk / limited or
  transparency-only / minimal) with the obligation attached to each and what
  sits on what. **Source:** artificialintelligenceact.eu high-level summary
  (T0).
  - `power-matrix` — who can actually do what to whom. **Data needed:**
  bodies (Commission AI Office, national competent authorities, notified
  bodies, market-surveillance authorities) crossed with obligation classes,
  each cell a control state. **Source:** artificialintelligenceact.eu
  responsibilities pages for the Commission and for Member States (T0).
  *(Published once, in the politics rewrites — the two above carry the
  never-published floor.)*
  - Supporting, non-drawn: `number-sense` on 3% of turnover made concrete
  against a named provider's revenue.
- **est. read time:** 6 min
