## C-01 · India's top football division was one contract, and the contract ran out

- **status:** open
- **why now:** On 19 August 2026 the AIFF confirmed the Indian Super League's
2026-27 season will run with **13 clubs** in a double round-robin, after
Diamond Harbour FC missed the deadline for the second instalment of its
participation fee — a club that had just earned its place by winning the
2025-26 Indian Football League after three straight promotions. Jamshedpur
FC's owner had already withdrawn a month earlier. That confirmation closes a
cycle that began when the Master Rights Agreement between the AIFF and
Football Sports Development Limited — signed in 2010 — expired in December
2025, leaving the league with no commercial engine. The 2025-26 season was
salvaged as a **13-match single round-robin**, won by East Bengal on 26
points; on 8 July 2026 the AIFF and the clubs announced a **club-led
commercial model** in which the clubs hold the commercial rights and the
federation keeps only the regulatory role. The new season starts 10 October.
- **angle:** The conventional frame is "Indian football is badly run." The
Parallax frame is narrower and more useful: the ISL was never a league in the
institutional sense. It was a **commercial agreement with fixtures attached**.
One private contract, signed in 2010 between the federation and a single
media company, supplied the broadcast, the sponsorship and the central
operations — so when it lapsed on a calendar date, the country's top division
simply stopped, and the fix was for the clubs to buy back the thing they
thought they were already playing in. The issue rebuilds the structure: what
the federation actually owned, what the rights-holder owned, what a club
owned, and where the money entered and left. Then it shows the cost in the
only units a reader can feel — a season cut from home-and-away to thirteen
one-off matches, a promoted club that won its way up and then could not pay
its way in, and a league that shrank while its fixture list grew back. The
structural revelation: promotion in India is a sporting result that a
participation fee can overrule, and that is a design choice, not an accident.

## C-02 · The club the models rated worst is third, and that is the model working

- **status:** open
- **why now:** Hull City came up through the 2026 play-offs on **73 points**
against an Opta expected-points figure of **53.1** — a total bettered only by
bottom-placed Sheffield Wednesday in that Championship season. Opta's
pre-season supercomputer then made Hull the most likely of the three promoted
clubs to go down, at **38.7%** (Ipswich 31.2%, Coventry 27.9%). Five
gameweeks into 2026-27 Hull are unbeaten and third on eight points from four
— a start only Nottingham Forest (1994-95) and Bolton (2001-02) have beaten
among promoted sides — while Aston Villa became the first club in Premier
League history to fail to score in the opening three matches of back-to-back
campaigns and Tottenham went five games without a league goal.
- **angle:** The conventional frame is "the model got Hull wrong." The Parallax
frame is that a model never said one thing. Opta ran **10,000 simulations**
and reported a *distribution*; 38.7% relegation means Hull survive in three
runs out of five, and the September table is one draw from that hat, not a
verdict on the hat. The issue teaches the reader to read an interval instead
of a number: how expected goals is built, what it deliberately throws away
(who took the shot, how the keeper was set), why a team can beat its expected
points two seasons running without that being proof of anything yet, and how
many matches it takes before the table stops being mostly noise. It lands on
the thing a cricket-watching Indian reader already half-knows from a batting
average after four innings. The structural revelation: the outlier everyone
quotes as the model's failure is the shape the model predicted, and the number
people argue about was never the claim.

## C-03 · The Premier League changed its spending rule the year its losses grew sevenfold

- **status:** chosen
- **why now:** The 2026-27 season, now five gameweeks old, is the **first** in
which the Premier League's Squad Cost Ratio and Sustainability and Systemic
Resilience rules actually bind, replacing the Profitability and Sustainability
Rules. Clubs may spend **85% of football revenue** on squad costs (wages,
player amortisation, agents' fees), with a multi-year allowance of **30%**, a
one-season ceiling of **115%** that draws a levy and eats the next season's
headroom, and a red threshold carrying points deductions; a separate positive
equity test tightens from **90%** this season to 85% and then 80%. The vote
passed **14-6**, exactly the two-thirds minimum. Deloitte's 2026 Annual Review
of Football Finance, covering 2024/25, is the backdrop: aggregate pre-tax
losses of **£948m** against £135m the season before, with eight clubs in
operating profit where thirteen had been, while the wages-to-revenue ratio
barely moved (65%, from 64%).
- **angle:** The conventional frame is "new financial fair play, clubs must
behave." The Parallax frame is what changing the *denominator* does. PSR
capped a loss in pounds; SCR caps a **ratio of your own revenue** — so the
same rule permits a very different wage bill at two clubs, and the club with
the bigger commercial and broadcast base is handed a larger legal budget by
the rule that is supposed to restrain it. The issue takes the reader through
one club's compliance arithmetic step by step, then shows where the revenue
actually comes from across the league, because that mix is what sets each
club's ceiling. It also names the thing the Deloitte numbers say out loud: the
losses did not come from wages, which held steady, but from player and asset
trading — the exact activity the new ratio counts. The structural revelation:
a percentage cap is a ranking system wearing a rulebook, and it locks the
order of the league in the season it starts.
- **suggested kinds** (drawn graphics, each with its data and the allowlisted
source that carries it):
  - `channel-ternary` — *never published.* Every club placed by its revenue
  split across matchday, broadcast and commercial, three shares summing to

  club revenue tables in **Deloitte's Annual Review of Football Finance 2026**
  (T3), cross-checked against **UEFA ECFIL** (T0) for the European picture.
  - `margin-bullets` — *never published.* The compliance dashboard: squad
  cost ratio against its 85% requirement, the one-season 115% ceiling, the
  positive equity ratio against 90%, wages-to-revenue against the 65% league
  average — each measurement against its own requirement, in mixed units.
  Data: thresholds from the **premierleague.com** rules explainer (T0);
  club actuals from Deloitte (T3) and **The Swiss Ramble**'s club-by-club
  reading of published accounts (T7).
  - `benchmark-chart` — the 20 clubs ranked on wages-to-revenue as
  horizontal bars with the 85% line drawn across them and one club

  Data: Deloitte ARFF 2026 (T3) and Swiss Ramble (T7).
