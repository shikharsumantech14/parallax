## C-02 · The rule built to stop this, and the case that almost broke it

- **status:** open
- **why now:** The Associated Party Transaction rules — tightened in
  December 2021 specifically to catch state-backed sponsorship inflation
  after Newcastle's takeover — were challenged by City as unlawful under
  competition law; a 2024 tribunal found parts of the regime (notably the
  exclusion of low-interest shareholder loans) unlawful, and City and the
  league only settled the dispute in September 2025, with City accepting
  the rules as "valid and binding." The settlement is barely a year old
  and the underlying mechanism — how "fair market value" gets decided —
  is still the load-bearing part of the whole financial-rules system.
- **angle:** Parallax shows the APT rules not as a fixed wall but as a
  rule that bent under a legal challenge built on the language of
  competition law rather than football ethics — City called the 14-of-20
  majority requirement "tyranny of the majority" and won a partial ruling
  on shareholder loans before settling. The structural revelation: a rule
  written to stop one specific ownership model can be dismantled clause
  by clause using the same market logic that made the ownership model
  possible in the first place.
- **suggested kinds:**
  - `vote-flow` **[never-published]** — the 14-of-20 majority vote

    The Pitch (T4) reporting on the vote; Premier League's own rulebook
    on majority thresholds (T0).
  - `arch-stack` — the APT framework as a layered system: league rule →

## C-04 · The clubs write the rules, then some of them sue over them

- **status:** open
- **why now:** The Premier League's Squad Cost Ratio/SSR system that
  replaces PSR from 2026/27 was approved by a vote of the 20 clubs in
  November 2025 — the same body of clubs whose own disputes (City's APT
  challenge, the 115 charges) drove the rule changes and now face the
  finished product. Meanwhile the UK's Football Governance Act 2025 has
  created an Independent Football Regulator explicitly because
  self-regulation — clubs voting on the rules that bind them — produces
  "inherently conflicted" governance.
- **angle:** Parallax steps back from the City case entirely and asks who
  actually runs the Premier League's financial rules: a league in which
  the 20 clubs vote on their own constraints, litigate the results, and
  now hand the whole design over to a state regulator built specifically
  because that self-regulatory loop kept producing disputes like this
  one. The structural revelation: the City case isn't an aberration in
  the system — it's what the system looks like when a club with enough
  legal resource tests a rulebook that its own members wrote and voted
  on.
