# Reader panel: Space junk now *grows* even if nobody launches

- **Draft:** src/content/issues/2026-04-24-kessler-cascade/index.mdx
- **Storyboard:** research/space/2026-09-14-kessler-cascade-storyboard.md
- **Pass:** first (after draft)
- **Read:** 2026-09-15
- **Verdict:** REVISE

Three mechanical fixes, none of them structural. The argument is taught, the
hero does its job, the quiz is answered correctly by all four readers, and no
section falls below 4 for anyone except one: section 5 is a 3 for Sana. That
single score is what holds the PASS back, and it is bought with one rewritten
sentence in a field that is already there.

Two notes on the list.

**"The floor."** This is the sharpest finding in the report. The follow-up is
the only plain-language landing a reader gets after a 44-word agency sentence,
and it spends its twelve words on a metaphor used twice with no concrete thing
attached. It also collides with the issue's own subject: a reader who has spent
four sections thinking in altitudes meets "the floor" and reaches for a height
before reaching for a baseline. Aarav, Meera and Sana all re-read it, and for
Sana it is the reason section 5 scores a 3 rather than a 4 — the quote went
past her and the sentence meant to catch her did not.

**"Near-miss risk."** Used twice, two sections apart, never glossed. Karthik
reads it as collision-risk contribution and moves on. Aarav and Sana convert it
to "close calls", which is close enough that the gist survives. Meera stops,
because a percentage of a risk with no denominator is the one shape she is
trained to distrust. It costs one re-read in two places and it is the cheapest
fix in the issue.

## The hook

> "Last year one company's satellites dodged 300,000 times. The junk you never see has never once been cleaned up."

**It stops the thumb for all four.** It has the number, the "you", and the
twist lands on "never once", which is the strongest three words in the head.

- **Sana** — stops. 300,000 is the scroll-stopper and "never once been cleaned
  up" is the turn she would screenshot. This is the hook working as designed.
- **Aarav** — stops. "Dodged" without an object is clarified by the next clause,
  so no re-read.
- **Meera** — stops, then pays one beat for "one company's". She wants the name
  and does not get it until section 6. Coyness reads as evasion to her for about
  a second, then the sentence pays her back.
- **Karthik** — stops, and immediately wants the fleet size to judge whether
  300,000 is a lot. He gets it in section 7 (10,074), which is two sections
  later than he asks.

**What it lacks: a thing to picture.** Number, "you" and twist are all present.
Nothing physical is. No reader can see the junk, its size, or its speed anywhere
in the head or the first three sections. This is a note, not a fix request —
adding a physical image would need a fact the record does not carry, and the
80-word counter is at 79.

**The dek** — "Two tests, two altitudes. One cloud cleared. One stays past 2100."

**Punchy, not staccato**, for three of four. It reads as three headline beats,
not as clipped prose, because it sits in a headline slot rather than a
paragraph — the gate's three-sentences-under-eight-words flag is correct
arithmetic and the wrong diagnosis here. Aarav and Sana read it at speed and it
sets them up for the chart exactly as intended. **Meera re-reads "Two tests"**,
because the noun is missing: tests of what, at that point in the page, could be
nuclear, missile or engine. She gets it in section 2. "One cloud cleared" works
only because the primer has already said "thousands of fragments" — the metaphor
is carried by the sentence before it, which is fine, and would not survive the
dek being read on its own in a share card.

## Findings on the six points

**1 · Does a cold reader come away understanding that altitude is the dial? Yes, all four.**
This is the issue's clearest success. Not one of the four finished thinking the
lesson was "space is crowded". Every retell of section 4 above is a statement
about height, and all four answered Q1 with altitude as the mechanism, not as a
detail. The hero states the finding five separate times — in the section title,
the intro's question and answer, the caption, the authored `plain` line, and
both annotations — and the encoding agrees with it, so a reader who reads only
the bars reaches the same place as one who reads every word. The two annotations
are doing the heaviest lifting: "283 km. The air still reaches. Gone in two
years." against "865 km. Nothing slows it. Still there after 2100." is the
thesis in nineteen words at the exact spot the eye is already resting. Keep
both untouched.

One completeness note that did not cost a score. Three of the five bars say what
is left ("~2,800 left", "0.3% left", "nearly all gone"); two do not (the 2009
collision's "~2,900 fragments" and the rocket stage's "700+ fragments, at ~800
km" give a count with no remainder). Meera noticed the pattern break. The
reader's inference — high means still up there — is the correct one and is
guided by the annotations, so the gap is safe. Say so in the next pass only if a
sourced remainder exists for either event.

**2 · Mission Shakti: fair to all four, with one proximity risk.**
Nobody felt flattered and nobody felt got at. The physics credit is explicit and
factual ("Fired deliberately low, at 283 km", "nearly all gone"), and the
political fact arrives four sections later without an editorial verb. Aarav's
reading is the one to note: he reads "fired deliberately low" as competence, and
then reads the abstention as India keeping its options open, and neither reading
carries heat. Sana takes the highlight as a small point of pride and moves on.
Karthik and Meera both register the abstention as information rather than as a
charge, largely because the sentence ends on the resolution's weakness rather
than on India's choice: "India abstained, and the resolution binds nobody" puts
the last word on the instrument, not the country. That clause is what makes the
section fair, and it should not be cut for length.

**The risk is adjacency, not wording.** The three facts run: "The vote was 155
for and 9 against. China and Russia were two of those 9. India abstained, and
the resolution binds nobody." A fast reader on a phone can blur the third
sentence into the second and file India with the two "against". All four
panellists know what abstaining means, so none of them made the error — but they
are reading carefully because they were asked to. The move, if you want to close
it, is to give "abstained" its own half-clause of meaning rather than to
separate the sentences, and it costs about four words out of the 160 the issue
has spare.

**3 · The two cuts: no reader missed either, but the close is colder than the peak.**

*Where the junk sits (the cut `orbital-shells`).* **No hole.** Not one reader
asked for a picture of the bands, and the reason is that the hero already
answers "where" in the only sense the argument needs: five real altitudes on one
axis, with what happened at each. A five-band density diagram would have added a
second altitude graphic saying something the bars already say.

What three of four readers *do* ask, and the issue never answers, is **how much
is up there in total**. Aarav, Meera and Sana all reached for a stock figure and
found only flows and fleets: 10,074 Starlink satellites, 7,473 objects added in
2024, and counts per event. "How big is the problem" is the first question a
reader brings to this subject and the issue answers it with rates instead of a
total. That is a sourcing decision, not a drafting one, and restoring the cut
kind would not have fixed it either — its density values were editorial. Flag to
the operator: one published total from the ESA report would close it, and that
is a research job.

*The close (the cut ClearSpace-1 passage).* **It ends adequately, and it peaks
one section early.** Asked what they would repeat to a friend, three of four
readers named the **zero tile in section 7** — "nothing has ever been cleaned up,
and one mission got within 15 metres and then left". Only Meera named the
paradox title. The issue's hardest, most repeatable fact therefore sits in the
second-last section, and the last thing the reader meets is a vote count and a
sentence about a resolution that binds nobody. That is a legitimate flat CALM
close and every reader could retell it, so it passes the gate. It does not land.

Related, and worth the operator's read: **no reader could say what breaks for
them personally if the cascade runs.** The hook promises "the junk you never
see", and the stakes the body delivers are institutional — fuel, fines, votes,
a station moving. The dropped GPS claim was the old issue's only answer to "what
do I lose", and dropping it was right because it was wrong. Nothing replaced it.
The issue's answer to "why care" is now "you already pay", which is honest and
cool. Whether that is enough is an editorial call, not a comprehension defect,
and any replacement would need a new sourced claim.

**4 · Dek and hook.** Covered above. Dek reads punchy to three of four, with
Meera re-reading "Two tests" for the missing noun. Hook stops all four thumbs,
Sana's included, and lacks only a thing to picture.

**5 · Karthik and the jargon: the jargon-buster carries him, and the issue does not assume orbital mechanics.**
The best decision in the draft is one that does not appear in it: **"conjunction"
is never used.** The issue says "dodge", "move", "course change" and "avoidance
move" instead of glossing a term it does not need. "Collision avoidance" and
"decay" are both glossed in one sentence each, in the section before the hero
leans on them. "Kessler syndrome" is glossed in section 3 and then met again in
ESA's own words in section 5, which is a restatement in the best sense. The
orbital regimes are reduced to a single band with its kilometre range in the
primer, and no acronym survives anywhere in reader-facing prose.

Three places the issue still assumes, in order of cost:

- **"near-miss risk"** (section 2 annotation, section 7 tile) — twice, never
  glossed, and the only undefined quantity in the issue.
- **"rocket stage"** (section 2 label and note, section 4 bar label) — the note
  "It had just released a batch of new satellites" tells a reader what it was
  doing but never what it is. Sana inferred correctly; Aarav took a beat.
- **The $150,000 fine arrives without the rule it enforces.** The reader is told
  someone was fined "for leaving a satellite" and is never told that leaving one
  is against a rule, or whose rule. Karthik and Sana both stopped here. It also
  sets up the friction in point 6.

Ordering, not vocabulary, causes the one real jargon problem: **section 1
asserts that the air drags fragments down, and section 3 is where the reader
learns there is any air up there at all.** Aarav and Sana both blinked at it,
because "space is a vacuum" is the thing they already believe and the draft
overturns it in passing, two sections before it explains it. Karthik, an
engineer, read straight through. This is the cheapest high-value fix in the
issue and it is free against the 80-word gate: `check-prose.mjs`'s `before`
loop (lines 251–258) adds only `eyebrow`, `title` and `intro` for section 1 and
then breaks, because `you-think` is not in `TEXT_ONLY`. Section 1's
`data.actually.text`, `data.note` and `caption` are **outside the 79 of 80**
entirely, and spend only from the ~160 words of headroom the issue has against
the 1,100 ceiling.

**6 · The paradox: two genuine forces, with the wrong evidence under one of them.**
The framing is right and the title does most of the work — "You pay to dodge.
Nobody pays to leave." is six words and all four readers retold the section from
it alone. The two `statement` lines are in real tension: the cost of the junk
falls on whoever is still flying, never on whoever made it. That is a paradox by
the catalog's own test, and it is a clear improvement on two examples sitting
side by side.

**Side B's `detail` proves a neighbouring claim.** The statement is "Nobody is
billed for the junk they leave behind." The evidence offered is the December
2022 UN vote on destructive anti-satellite tests, 155 to 9, non-binding. A
non-binding test ban is not a bill: it supports "nobody is *bound*", which is a
different sentence. The billing evidence exists, but it is in the previous
section (the single $150,000 fine) and in this section's own intro ("one fine
ever issued"), so the reader has to carry it across a section break to make side
B stand up. Meera and Karthik both noticed the seam; Aarav and Sana absorbed the
UN fact as general non-enforcement and did not.

There is a second-order friction under it. Section 7 says a fine **was** levied,
and section 8 says **nobody** is billed. The reconciliation — once, ever, to one
operator — is present in both places but is never said in the sentence that
needs it. Karthik, who read the tile carefully, stopped on this. Direction: make
side B's detail carry the billing fact it asserts, or align the statement to
what the UN vote actually proves. Do not do both; the section only has room for
one.

## What would fix it (direction only, never the sentence)

Three fixes, in order of what they buy. All three are field-level edits; none
touches the spine, the hero, the annotations or the 79-of-80 head.

1. **Section 5's `followup` is the only fix that changes the verdict.** "The
   floor", used twice as the sole plain landing after a 44-word agency sentence,
   is an abstraction where the reader needs a concrete thing, and it competes
   with the issue's own altitude frame. Give the field the job the contract's
   rule 5 gives it: restate, in the reader's words, what the quote just said,
   and let the second sentence point forward. Same field, same length, no new
   fact. This is what takes Sana from 3 to 4 and the issue from REVISE to PASS.

2. **Gloss "near-miss risk" once, at the tile, not at the annotation.** The
   annotation in section 2 is capped at 12 words and is using 10 of them well.
   The section 7 tile note is the right home and it is the storyboard's own
   designated slack (§8g: row 7's notes pay first, ≤10 words → ≤7 saves 18).
   The tile note currently reads at 10 words, so the gloss is funded from inside
   section 7 and costs the issue nothing.

3. **Move the air, not the words, in section 1.** The draft leans on atmospheric
   drag two sections before it explains it, and the explanation it needs is
   already written in section 3's "Decay" gloss. Either let section 1's
   `actually` panel stop leaning on the mechanism, or let it carry three words of
   it. Funding is confirmed, not assumed: section 1's `data` fields and `caption`
   sit outside the words-before-first-graphic counter (`check-prose.mjs` lines
   251–258, which breaks after section 1's `intro` because `you-think` is
   visual), so this spends from the ~160-word gap to the 1,100 ceiling and the
   79-of-80 head is untouched.

**Fourth, if the operator wants it, and it is a judgement call rather than a
comprehension fix:** side B of the paradox evidences "nobody is bound" while
stating "nobody is billed" (point 6). Either the detail or the statement should
move to meet the other. And one line under it: the fact every reader says they
would repeat is the zero tile in section 7, not the closer — if the close should
be the thing repeated, that is where the zero belongs.

**⚠ verify (flagged, not judged — the verifier's lane):** section 7, "7,473
objects added to orbit in 2024 · A single-year jump, mostly from things breaking
apart." The issue's own 2024 break-ups account for roughly 900 catalogued pieces
(180+ and 700+), so "mostly" does not close against the figures on the same
page. Karthik did the arithmetic and stopped. Whatever the source says, the word
is doing more work than the issue's own numbers support.
