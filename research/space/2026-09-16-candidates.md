## C-01 · Three whole satellites now fall out of the sky every day, and that is the fix working

- **status:** open
- **why now:** ESA published its Annual Space Environment Report 2026 on
**14 September 2026**, two days before this run. It is the space desk's
biggest annual data release, and this edition added a brand-new metric:
the on-ground casualty risk from reentries. It also reports the Space
Environment Health Index jumping from about 4 to about 50 in a single
year, and it shortened its own projection horizon from 200 years to 100.
Six days earlier the White House policy target (NSPM-17, 20 August 2026)
set a goal of over 1,000 launches **and reentries** a year on American
soil by 2030.
- **angle:** Everyone has been taught that the space junk problem is about
what stays up. The 2026 report's new number is about what comes down.
1,200 intact objects reentered in 2025, more than three a day, and the
reason that count is climbing is not failure. It is compliance. About 90%
of rocket bodies in low orbit now clear the 25-year disposal rule and more
than half come down under control, so the mitigation regime is working as
designed and its designed output is a permanent stream of hardware falling
through the atmosphere. The structural revelation sits in the safety
standard: the 1-in-10,000 casualty threshold used by the US, France and
ESA is written **per reentry event**, and nobody wrote the rule for a
world with 1,200 events a year. A per-event limit does not add up to a
population limit. The closing proof that nobody actually knows what
survives the fire is ESA's Draco mission, a satellite carrying 200 sensors
and 4 cameras whose entire job is to record its own destruction from the
inside and fire a 40 cm capsule out of the fireball with the data.
- **follows:** `2026-04-24-kessler-cascade`. That issue argued junk grows in
orbit even if nobody launches. This one is the opposite direction of
travel and does not restate it: the disposal regime built partly in answer
to Kessler moved the problem out of orbit and into the atmosphere and onto
the ground. Kessler is referenced in one line, not re-explained.
- **suggested kinds (drawn graphics, data and source named):**
  1. `launch-stats` — *never published.* Reentries per year, 2015–2025,
    each year's bar split controlled vs uncontrolled. Data: ESA Space
     Environment Report 2026 plus the DISCOSweb Space Environment Statistics
     portal (T1, machine-readable series). Space signature, hero-capable.
  2. `orbital-shells` — *never published.* Altitude bands with their
    density, operators and persistence, showing which shells atmospheric
     drag empties in years and which hold hardware for centuries. Data:
     DISCOSweb statistics broken down by altitude band (T1). This is the
     kind's exact DATA shape, `{shells:[{altitude, band, density, operators,  persistence}]}`.
  3. `descent-profile` — *never published.* Altitude against time for one
    real uncontrolled rocket-body reentry, with breakup events flagged.
     Data: Aerospace Corporation CORDS reentry and breakup studies (T3/T6)
     plus ESA's reentry prediction service. **Hardest capture of the three**
     and flagged again in notes.

## C-02 · The space station's retirement date was set by a contract, not by the station

- **status:** chosen
- **why now:** GAO published **GAO-26-107805** on 17 June 2026, finding that
NASA must decide **in 2027** whether a commercial station will be ready
before the ISS retires in 2030, or whether to change plans. NASA planned
to award one or more Space Act Agreements during 2026 and floated an
alternative government-owned core module in March 2026, and SpaceNews
reported in September 2026 that the commercial station partnerships are
going international. The decision window is open right now.
- **angle:** The station is usually discussed as if it were an old car that
will fail one day. The schedule says otherwise. The retirement runs on a
sequence of dates that were chosen: natural decay plus Russian-segment
manoeuvres begin lowering the station in early-to-mid 2028, the US Deorbit
Vehicle launches in 2029, and the reentry burn comes at the end of 2030 or
early 2031. Once the deorbit vehicle flies, the clock is mechanical. The
structural point is that the 2027 assessment is really a decision about
which risk to accept, a gap in continuous human presence in low orbit
(unbroken since November 2000) or the cost of extending a station NASA has
already told Congress it will stop funding. GAO's own recommendation is
the tell: it asked NASA to **write down the factors** it will use, because
they were not documented. A decision of that size had no stated criteria.

## C-03 · Roman's mirror is the same width as Hubble's and it will cover the sky 1,000 times faster

- **status:** open
- **why now:** NASA activated the Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope's Wide
Field Instrument on **15 September 2026**, one day before this run, and
checked out the Coronagraph. The day before that, 14 September, NASA
reported that an unusually accurate first mid-course correction plus other
fuel savings left Roman with propellant for **at least 22 years** of
science, more than double the planned lifetime. The telescope launched on
30 August 2026 and is on its way to Sun-Earth L2. First science images are
expected early 2027, so the instrument story is live now and the results
story is not yet available to anyone.
- **angle:** The reader's model of a better telescope is a bigger mirror.
Roman's primary mirror is 2.4 metres, the same width as Hubble's, and it
was donated hardware rather than a new build. Everything that makes Roman
different sits behind the mirror: 18 infrared detectors totalling 300
megapixels across a sensing area about the size of a laptop screen, giving
a field of view at least 100 times Hubble's and letting it survey up to
1,000 times faster at similar sensitivity and resolution. The shift that
follows is the real subject. When survey speed rises that far, the
bottleneck stops being telescope time and becomes data and archiving, and
the mission's limit stops being optics and becomes fuel, which is why a
three-minute burn executed well was worth more than a decade of extra
science. The perspective shift is that a modern observatory is a
logistics achievement wearing an optics costume.
- **suggested kinds (drawn graphics, data and source named):**
  1. `lagrange-map` — *never published.* The five Sun-Earth Lagrange
    points with Roman's orbit at L2, about 1.5 million km out. Data: JPL
     Horizons (T1) for the geometry, plus NASA SVS "Roman Telescope Launch
     and Orbit at L2" (T0) for the mission's own trajectory. Space
     signature kind, and this is precisely its subject.
  2. `benchmark-chart` — Field of view, or survey speed, for Roman
    against Hubble, JWST and Euclid on one metric, Roman highlighted, with
     a reference line at Hubble. Data: NASA "Why the Roman Space Telescope?"

  3. `moore-ladder` — *never published.* Data returned per day by
