## 1. Structural argument (the Parallax take)

You assume the International Space Station is being retired because it is
wearing out: cracks in the Russian transfer tunnel, air leaks, twenty-six years
of thermal cycling. That is the story the pictures tell. The structure underneath
is different. The station's end date is a **scheduling artefact**, not an
engineering verdict. Five agencies signed up to operate it "through 2030"
(Russia only through 2028); NASA bought a single $843 million vehicle from
SpaceX to push it down; that vehicle must be delivered by 1 May 2029 and must fly
roughly eighteen months before the final burn; and once it is docked the rest is
arithmetic, not judgement. The mechanism that produces the gap between surface
and structure is **procurement lead time**. The reason NASA must decide in 2027
is not that the station will fail in 2027 — it is that a replacement station
needs certification, and additional cargo and crew flights need to be bought
three years ahead. GAO's June 2026 report exposes the tell: it had to recommend
that NASA **write down** the factors it will use to decide, because NASA had not
documented them. A decision that is not written down is a decision the calendar
makes for you. India is the useful mirror here: ISRO flew a Gaganyatri to this
station in 2025 as a stepping stone to its own, at 400 km, while the station he
visited was already on a delivery schedule to the bottom of the Pacific.

## 2. Why now

**GAO-26-107805, published 17 June 2026.** The U.S. Government Accountability
Office reported that NASA must assess **in 2027** whether a commercial station
will be available before the ISS retires in 2030, and that as of **May 2026**
NASA **had not finalized its acquisition approach** for commercial stations. GAO
made two recommendations, both of which are about undocumented reasoning rather
than hardware: assess the likelihood and duration of a human-presence gap using
NASA's own risk-management process, and document the assessment process itself,
"including key assumptions, risks, and uncertainties."

Two supporting hooks, both dated and both weaker than the GAO report — use GAO as
the anchor:

- **March 2026** — NASA floated an alternative approach in which the government
  owns a core module and companies attach their own modules to it, a significant
  departure from the fully-commercial premise. *(Reported; see §9 for the
  sourcing caveat.)*
- **August 2026** — NASA's Commercial Destinations page was last updated,
  still naming four partners (Axiom Space, Blue Origin's Orbital Reef, Vast's
  Haven-1, Starlab) and still describing the programme as "a phased approach."

## 4. Key facts & data

### 4.1 The station as it is

- **Operating altitude:** 370–460 km (200–250 nautical miles); the FAQ gives
  415 km / 257 miles as the working figure — [NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/international-space-station-frequently-asked-questions/)
- **Inclination:** 51.6° — [NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/international-space-station-frequently-asked-questions/)
- **Mass:** over 400,000 kg (925,000 lb in the deorbit briefing) — [NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/international-space-station-frequently-asked-questions/) · [Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2024/07/17/nasa-spacex-provide-details-on-space-station-deorbit-vehicle/)
- **Pressurised volume:** ~900 m³ — [NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/international-space-station-frequently-asked-questions/)
- **Drag loss:** the station loses roughly **100 m of altitude a day** without reboost — [Planetary Society](https://www.planetary.org/articles/how-nasa-plans-to-deorbit-the-international-space-station)
- **Unboosted orbital lifetime:** one to two years — [NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/international-space-station-frequently-asked-questions/)
- **People:** over 290 individuals from 26 countries; five partner agencies — [NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/international-space-station-frequently-asked-questions/)
- **Continuous human presence:** began 2 November 2000 — [SpaceNews](https://spacenews.com/on-the-eve-of-the-isss-25th-anniversary-humanity-stares-down-a-gap-in-its-presence-in-space/). As of 17 September 2026 that is **~9,450 days (25 years, ~10½ months)** — **[COMPUTED, not sourced]**: derived by the researcher from the start date. Treat as arithmetic, not a citation; the drafter should recompute for the publication date.
- **The record it beat:** Mir's 10 years of uninterrupted habitation, September 1989 to August 1999, not surpassed until October 2010 — [NASA History](https://www.nasa.gov/history/35-years-ago-launch-of-mir-space-stations-first-module/)

### 4.2 The vehicle that sets the clock

- **Contract:** single-award, SpaceX, total potential value **$843 million**. NASA takes ownership after development and operates it in flight — [NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-selects-international-space-station-us-deorbit-vehicle/)
- **Propellant:** 35,000 lb / **16,000 kg** — [Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2024/07/17/nasa-spacex-provide-details-on-space-station-deorbit-vehicle/)
- **Engines:** **46 Draco** — 16 for attitude control, **30 for the deorbit burns**, the 30 carried in an extended trunk — [Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2024/07/17/nasa-spacex-provide-details-on-space-station-deorbit-vehicle/)
- **Mass:** over 30,000 kg — [Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2024/07/17/nasa-spacex-provide-details-on-space-station-deorbit-vehicle/)
- **Trunk:** twice as long as a standard Dragon's, **six times** the propellant, **3–4×** the power — [Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2024/07/17/nasa-spacex-provide-details-on-space-station-deorbit-vehicle/)
- **Launcher:** needs a heavier class of rocket than Falcon 9, procured separately **at least three years before launch** — [Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2024/07/17/nasa-spacex-provide-details-on-space-station-deorbit-vehicle/)
- **Delivery:** desired 1 August 2028, **required 1 May 2029** — [Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2024/07/17/nasa-spacex-provide-details-on-space-station-deorbit-vehicle/)
- **Launch timing:** roughly **eighteen months before the final re-entry burn** — [Spaceflight Now](https://spaceflightnow.com/2024/07/17/nasa-spacex-provide-details-on-space-station-deorbit-vehicle/)
- **Fate:** "expected to destructively breakup as part of the re-entry process" — [NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/nasa-selects-international-space-station-us-deorbit-vehicle/)

### 4.3 The money

- **ISS operating cost:** about **$3 billion a year**, roughly a third of NASA's human spaceflight budget; extension beyond 2024 costed at $3–4 billion a year — [NASA OIG](https://oig.nasa.gov/office-of-inspector-general-oig/ig-22-005/)
- **FY2021 commercial-destination funding:** Congress authorised **$17 million** against the **$150 million** NASA said it needed — [NASA OIG](https://oig.nasa.gov/office-of-inspector-general-oig/ig-22-005/)
- **Commercial LEO Development Program, FY2026–2030:** NASA projected **$2.1 billion**; **$1–1.5 billion** of it for C3DO across **a minimum of two** awards — [SpaceNews](https://spacenews.com/nasa-releases-details-on-revised-next-phase-of-commercial-space-station-development/)

### 4.4 The decision, per GAO-26-107805

- NASA works with **six** U.S. companies on commercial stations — [GAO](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-107805)
- **As of May 2026 NASA had not finalized its acquisition approach**; it planned to award Space Act Agreements in 2026 followed by certification contracts — [GAO](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-107805)
- The 2027 assessment turns on several factors including "sufficient time for NASA to secure funding for additional transportation vehicles to support ongoing ISS operations" — [GAO](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-107805)
- **Recommendation 1:** assess "the likelihood and duration of a gap in continuous capability or human presence in LEO" using NASA's risk management process — [GAO](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-107805)
- **Recommendation 2:** document "the assessment process that NASA will use to make an informed decision" on ISS retirement, "including key assumptions, risks, and uncertainties" — [GAO](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-107805)
- **Phase 2 = C3DO** (Commercial Destinations – Development and Demonstration Objectives), funded Space Act Agreements rather than fixed-price contracts, culminating in a demonstration of **four people for at least 30 days**, target **no later than 2030**; certification deferred to a Phase 3 using contracts and full-and-open competition — [SpaceNews](https://spacenews.com/nasa-releases-details-on-revised-next-phase-of-commercial-space-station-development/)
- **Named partners:** Axiom Space, Blue Origin (Orbital Reef), Vast (Haven-1), Starlab — [NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/humans-in-space/commercial-space/low-earth-orbit-economy/commercial-destinations-in-low-earth-orbit/)

### 4.7 Jargon rows to append to `research/_voice/jargon.md`

Append only — never rewrite another desk's rows.

| Term | Plainest gloss the sources support |
|---|---|
| USDV (U.S. Deorbit Vehicle) | The tug NASA bought to push the station down. A Dragon capsule with a much longer boot full of fuel. |
| Deorbit | Slowing something down on purpose so the air drags it out of the sky. |
| Space Act Agreement | A funding handshake, not a purchase order. NASA pays towards a company's own project instead of buying a product. |
| Certification | NASA's sign-off that a vehicle is safe enough to put its own people in. |
| Delta-v | How much speed change a manoeuvre costs, in metres per second. The fuel bill, written as speed. |
| LEO (low Earth orbit) | The band of space from roughly 200 to 2,000 km up, where the station and most satellites fly. |
| Reboost | Firing engines to push the station back up after air drag pulls it down. |
| Point Nemo | The spot in the South Pacific furthest from any land. Where dead spacecraft are aimed. |
| Bharatiya Antariksh Station | India's own planned space station. First module committed for December 2028. |
