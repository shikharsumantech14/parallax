## C-01 · The Orbit That Can't Forget

- **status:** open
- **why now:** ESA released the tenth edition of its annual Space
  Environment Report on 1 May 2026 (data through end-2024): ~40,000
  catalogued objects, ~11,000 active payloads, and an estimated 50,000+
  objects over 10 cm and 1.2 million over 1 cm. The report's headline is
  no longer a warning about the future — it states plainly that on current
  behaviour "the risk level passes beyond the point of sustainability,"
  and that active debris removal is now *required* to keep certain orbits
  usable. Within the most crowded altitude bands, the density of active
  satellites is now the same order of magnitude as dead debris.
- **angle:** Most readers picture space junk as scattered litter you could
  sweep up. The structural truth is the opposite: low Earth orbit behaves
  like a memory that can't be erased. Even with zero new launches, ESA's
  models show the debris count keeps climbing, because fragmentation
  events generate new objects faster than atmospheric drag can pull old
  ones down — the runaway feedback Donald Kessler described in 1978.
  Parallax shows orbit as a one-way ledger: every collision is a deposit
  that compounds, and the 550 km Starlink shell is now the most heavily
  mortgaged band in the sky. The shift is from "we have a cleanup problem"
  to "we crossed a threshold and the system now adds to itself."
- **suggested kinds:** orbit-globe (active constellations + debris bands
  by altitude/inclination on the dot-matrix Earth — the showcase data
  shape maps directly; add a debris-density band at 550–900 km),
  launch-stats (the launch-cadence curve that feeds the debris growth,
  2020–2026), data-readout (the ESA tile board: 40,000 / 50,000 / 1.2M /
  11,000), timeline (Kessler 1978 → Iridium-Cosmos 2009 → 2007 ASAT →
  2021 ASAT → 2026 report), paradox (zero launches, rising debris)

## C-03 · The Longest Way Home

- **why now:** Artemis II launched 1 April 2026 at 6:35 p.m. from Kennedy

  50 years. Commander Reid Wiseman, pilot Victor Glover, mission
  specialist Christina Koch and CSA's Jeremy Hansen reached 252,756 miles
  from Earth, surpassing the distance record Apollo 13's crew set in 1970,
  aboard Orion ("Integrity") on the SLS. With the crew freshly home (the
  flight is days-to-weeks old as of 3 June), the trajectory and its
  engineering are fully documented and ripe for a structural read.
- **angle:** The popular story is "America went back to the Moon." The
  structural story is the shape of the path itself. Artemis II flew a
  *free-return trajectory* — a figure-eight that uses lunar gravity to
  sling the capsule home with the engine off, the same survival geometry
  that saved Apollo 13. Parallax reads the mission as a lesson in orbital
  mechanics as risk management: the crew went *farther* than anyone in
  history precisely because the safest path was the longest one, looping
  beyond the Moon so physics, not a thruster, guaranteed the return. The
  shift is from "a flag-planting milestone" to "the geometry of coming
  back is the mission, and it's the same curve drawn in 1970."

## C-04 · The Asteroid We Talked Down

- **status:** chosen
- **why now:** On 5 March 2026 NASA and ESA announced that asteroid 2024
  YR4 — for a stretch the most dangerous near-Earth object catalogued in
  20 years — will *not* hit the Moon. JWST (NIRCam) observations on 18 and
  26 February 2026, anchored by Gaia stellar-position data, shrank the
  ~60-metre rock's lunar-impact odds for 22 December 2032 from a peak of
  ~4% to zero; it will pass beyond 20,000 km. The object was discovered in
  December 2024, briefly carried the highest Earth-impact probability ever
  formally recorded before Earth was ruled out, then migrated its risk to
  the Moon — a full risk-resolution arc that just closed.
- **angle:** Headlines treat each twist ("4% chance!" → "it'll miss!") as
  contradictory drama. Parallax reads 2024 YR4 as a portrait of how
  certainty is *manufactured*: the impact probability didn't swing because
  the asteroid changed — it swung because the error ellipse shrank as more
  photons arrived. The structural revelation is that a rising-then-
  vanishing impact percentage is the *expected* signature of a working
  planetary-defense system, not a failure of one. Parallax shows the
  observation arc as an uncertainty funnel: each telescope pass narrows
  the cone of where the rock can be in 2032 until the Moon falls outside
  it. The shift is from "scientists kept changing their minds" to "the
  number going up before it goes to zero is exactly what success looks
  like."
