- **Draft:** `src/content/issues/2026-06-04-asteroid-2024-yr4/index.mdx`
- **Storyboard:** `research/space/2026-09-14-asteroid-2024-yr4-storyboard.md`
- **Pass:** first (after draft)
- **Read:** 2026-09-15
- **Verdict:** **REVISE**

**One line:** the counter-intuitive core lands — nobody finishes this thinking the
rock changed course, and all four readers answer Q1 and Q3 correctly — but the
word carrying the lunar rise, *share*, is never opened, the `number-sense` ≈
column puts three unlike statements under one number, and two names arrive with
no role. Every one of those is a gloss or a split, not a rebuild.

### Gaps against the storyboard's model answers (checked after the fact)

1. **Q2's model answer runs "1.7%, then 3.8%, then 4.3%".** The draft carries

   Not a defect — the answer survives on two rungs.
2. **Q3's model answer says Webb "pinned the 2032 position two years before any
   ground telescope could."** That is **absent from the draft entirely.** The
   reader learns *that* Webb settled it, never *why only Webb could*. The beat

   "Then the rock fades from view" (event 4) → "two faint detections" → the
   almond. No sentence says no ground telescope could have done it. This is the
   single real storyboard-to-draft shortfall on the quiz.
3. **Q3's "among the faintest ever made of an asteroid"** is likewise gone. The
   storyboard's own fallback wording in §8c.3 carried it; the chosen wording
   does not.

## The hook

> **Title:** "The odds *moved*. The asteroid did not."
> **Hook:** "An asteroid's chance of hitting Earth once read 1 in 32. Six days
> later, 1 in 25,000. The rock you were watching never moved."

**It stops all four thumbs.** It carries all three things §3.12 asks for: a
number a reader can feel (1 in 32, not 3.1%), a "you" ("the rock you were
watching"), and a twist in the last clause. The title states the finding in
seven words and the italic on *moved* puts the stress on the right syllable.

- **Sana** — yes, and this is the strongest hook of the four rewrites so far for
  her specifically: "1 in 32" is a number she can put in a story, and the twist
  is in the final five words where she is still reading.
- **Aarav** — yes. Title and hook say the same thing at two zoom levels, and
  the hook pays for the repeat by supplying both numbers.
- **Meera** — yes, and §01 pays it off immediately, which is what keeps her.
- **Karthik** — yes. Mild snag: "the rock you were watching" is a fiction for a
  reader who was not watching; it costs nothing.

**One head-to-body mismatch.** The dek ("The number rose before it fell") and
the primer ("its odds of a hit rose, then fell to zero") both promise a **rise**
in the Earth odds. The timeline's first Earth figure is already the peak — the
rise is asserted in the head and never drawn in the body. It is defensible if
"the number" means the lunar arc, which the body does show, but no reader
resolves it that way on first pass. Low severity; one line to fix.

## The six things this read was asked to test

**1 · Does the counter-intuitive core survive a phone read? Mostly, and nobody
fails it dangerously.** No reader finishes thinking the rock changed course or
that scientists were guessing. The draft says "same orbit" or its equivalent
five times (§01 `actually.text`, §02 intro, §02 Jun 3 annotation, §05 intro,
§06 `followup`) and the annotation *"The risk moves to the Moon. The rock does
not."* sits on the exact mark where the misread would happen. **That is not a
BLOCK-level finding.** What does not land is the *arithmetic intuition*: the
chain's third link — "the range keeps shrinking with the Moon inside it, **so
the lunar share rises**" — turns on the word *share*, and nothing in the issue
says what the share is a share **of**. `jargon-buster` term 1 was the place to
install it and instead says "a reading off how well the orbit is known", which
is the same abstraction one level higher. Aarav, Meera and Karthik can retell
the chain correctly; only Meera and Karthik could defend it. Sana gets the move
and not the rise. Fix is one sentence of denominator, not a new section.

**2 · Karthik's skip test on *aasmaan*: PASSES, with a note.** Delete it —
*"It is still up there, on the same path it has always flown"* — and the English
says everything. Karthik loses nothing; his stumble in this issue is a name
(Gaia), not a Hindi word. It also passes the precision test: no number in the
sentence, nowhere near a caption or a source line, and it is the issue's only
Hindi word as ruled in §8c.5. **On the wince test it is borderline but clean.**
The reservation is the natural-word one: the same sentence already says "up
there", so *aasmaan* is the second way of saying sky in eleven words, and it
arrives cold in the last line of an otherwise all-English issue. It reads as
placed rather than spoken. That is a stylist's call, not a comprehension defect,
and the panel does not ask for it to come out.

**3 · Three glosses in one text-only section is one too many, and it is term 1
Aarav would drop.** He keeps **the error box** (it gives him a picture) and
**the 1% line** (it gives him a threshold — the only thing in the issue that
answers "is 4% a lot"). He drops **impact probability**, because he walked in
believing he already knew what a probability is and the definition offered adds
nothing he can hold: "written as a percentage" he had, and "a reading off how
well the orbit is known" he cannot repeat. The order compounds it — the
abstraction is defined first and the concrete thing that defines it comes
second. The useful half of term 1 (probability here reads the *knowledge*, not
the *danger*) belongs inside term 2, beside the box. The conceptual load itself
is not too heavy; its sequencing is.

**4 · The second framing helps the idea and the component's grammar fights
it.** "Thirty-one chances in thirty-two that nothing happens" is the right move
— it converts a frightening number into an almost-certainly-nothing, and it is
the one line that gives a reader permission to stop being scared. But
`NumberSense` renders every `equals` row behind a visible ≈ hanging off the big
3.1%, so a sighted phone reader meets three statements presented as three
equivalents of one number, and **only the first one is**. Row 2 is the
complement; row 3 is a different figure from a different date. The `note` under
each rescues it at 13px in `--muted`, under a 17.5px line. Meera and Karthik
both re-read here. **And no, "about one chance in 25,000" does not read as the
same kind of thing** — it is a *later reading*, not another way of saying the
same reading, and putting it third in an equivalence list is what makes it look
like one. It belongs out of the `equals` list or explicitly marked as the
after-number.

**5 · The close is satisfying, and yes — they know it is settled, not merely
current.** "On 22 December 2032 it arrives, and it misses" is flat, declarative
and final, and it is reinforced twice: timeline event 6 carries `state: now`
with the label "Lunar impact ruled out. Zero.", and `data-readout` tile 6 names
the single date every probability was ever a reading for. All four readers
finish knowing it is over. The anticlimax risk is **not** the absence of a
flourish — ending on NASA's own sentence is the right structural choice and
Meera and Karthik read it as the issue refusing to gild. The risk is the
sentence itself: "reflects improved precision in our understanding of where the
asteroid is expected to be in 2032" is precisely the register this rewrite
exists to translate, it is verbatim so it cannot be touched, and the `followup`
translates only its back half. Aarav and Sana take the landing and skip the
quote it is supposed to land on.

**6 · Seven names, under ration — but the almond reads as unsourced, and two
names arrive with no role.** The count is right and the ration is honoured. Two
findings against it:

- **The almond.** It renders inside a timeline note as *"It reflects 'about as
  much light as an almond at the distance of the Moon.'"* — quote marks, no
  speaker, in a note whose other sentence carries none. The marks announce that
  somebody said this and then decline to say who. §8c.4 ruled the two
  investigators **described, not named**; the draft describes nobody at all, so
  the ruling's second half never shipped. Aarav and Sana read past it; Meera
  and Karthik notice the orphan. As an *image* it works — badam is the right
  everyday object and it is the best register asset in the record — but it is
  a brightness comparison wearing a size comparison's clothes, and "reflects"
  is the only word doing that job.
- **Webb and Gaia.** §7 of the storyboard wrote a role phrase for each ("the
  space telescope that made the measurement nothing on the ground could"; "the
  mission whose star map every Webb measurement is made against"). Neither
  reached the draft. The issue never states that Webb is a telescope or that
  Gaia is anything at all. ATLAS, by contrast, is introduced correctly in the
  same section ("a survey telescope NASA funds") — which is what makes the
  other two conspicuous.

## What would fix it (direction only, never the sentence)

Ordered by what it buys per word spent. The first three are the ones that move
the verdict.

1. **§02, the Jun 3 note: open the word *share*.** It is the pivot of the whole
   issue and it is the only unexplained term in the draft that carries an
   argument. Say what the share is a share of, in the note itself or in
   `jargon-buster` term 1 — the reader needs to know there is a total being
   divided before "share" means anything. Sana's Q2 turns on this one move.
2. **§02, the Feb 24 note: "so 1.7% is left" needs the thing it is left from.**
   Same defect one event earlier, and it is the first sentence Aarav read twice.
   The residual is introduced before the reader has been shown that probability
   is distributed across targets at all.
3. **§04: the ≈ column is claiming three equivalences and only owns one.** Mark
   the complement as the complement and move the six-days-later reading out of
   the equals list, or give it a label that says it is a different date. The
   component prints ≈ on every row and cannot be told otherwise from the MDX.
4. **§03: reorder, and merge the useful half of term 1 into term 2.** The
   concrete box should define the abstract probability, not follow it. And "a
   reading off" is not plain English — it is the one idiom in a section whose
   whole job is plainness.
5. **§02, events 5 and 6: give Webb and Gaia the role phrases the storyboard
   already wrote.** One clause each. ATLAS shows how it is done three events
   earlier.
6. **§02, event 5: the almond needs a speaker or it needs to stop wearing quote
   marks.** Either give the two investigators the role phrase §8c.4 ruled for
   them, or let the comparison be the issue's own sentence with the source line
   carrying it.
7. **§05, tile 5: one distance, one unit.** Two agencies, three numbers and two
   unit systems in a twelve-word note, with the citation inside the sentence
   that §8h.3 forbade. Pick the figure the reader should carry; the source line
   holds who said it. *(Separately: `emphasis: "warn"` marks the good news in
   the alarm colour — worth a second look, though that is a design call.)*
8. **§06: the `followup` translates half the quote.** "Same path" answers
   "rather than a shift in its orbital path". Nothing answers "improved
   precision in our understanding", which is the harder half and the half that
   *is* the issue's thesis.
9. **The head promises a rise the body never draws.** Dek and primer both say
   the number rose; the timeline's first Earth figure is already the peak. One
   line, low severity.

### Two things the reader wanted and the draft does not owe them

Recorded so the next pass does not mistake them for defects.

- **"What would it actually have done?"** All four asked it; nothing answers it.
  The rock's size sits at 53–67 m with only its own width as a yardstick.
  Storyboard §5 ruled this deliberately — every human-scale comparison needs a
  second number the sources do not carry. It is a research gap, not a draft
  defect, and it should stay unanswered rather than be invented.
- **"Why could only Webb do it?"** See the Q3 gap above. This one *is* on the
  record (the almond quote and the fade from view are both in the sources) and
  is worth a clause if any words come free. ⚠ verify whether "two years before
  any ground telescope could" traces to the dossier before it is used.
